# Chapter 1: Why permission to enter does not yet mean someone must provide a seat

<a id="ch1-scope"></a>
This lecture examines how a study society connects entry, reservations, cancellation, points, and review. All rules, people, and data below come from synthetic teaching material. They are teaching hypotheticals, not current law or a record of an actual class or case. We reason only within this exercise's rules. No materials from preceding or subsequent classes are available, so the relationships needed to understand this lecture must be explained here.

<a id="ch1-whole-task"></a>
First place the eventual problem before us. G is a society member who reserves a seat on Monday for Wednesday 10:00–12:00 and receives successful confirmation. G later attempts cancellation; the premises close on Wednesday, and a points refund is ultimately involved. The sequence connects several questions: what did G acquire initially? Did that relationship later change? Which conditions trigger which consequences? How should an objection to the result be reviewed?

Begin at the starting point. Unless we distinguish 'may enter' from 'may require whom to provide what,' later analysis of cancellation, charges, and review will lose its object. Chapter 1 identifies how the relationship is established. Later developments will be assessed once the relevant rules and facts are available.

<a id="ch1-permission"></a>
## 1. Direct permission to enter toward the act of entering

Exercise Rule L1 first provides that registered members have permission to enter the public reading area, but that permission does not guarantee a seat. Together, these statements delimit the permission's content.

Registered members may enter, meaning that this rule does not prohibit their act of entry. It answers 'May the member enter?' It does not also answer 'Will a seat necessarily be available?' or designate someone else who must provide something for the member's entry. Rewriting permission to enter as a seat guarantee silently shifts its object from entering to supplying a seat.

<a id="ch1-student-objection"></a>
The synthetic material includes a student's objection: 'Is permission completely useless?' This objection deserves preservation because saying only that permission does not guarantee a seat may suggest that permission has no effect at all. It does have an effect: it excludes a prohibition on entry under this rule. That effect simply does not suffice to guarantee a different result: that a seat will be available. Being able to enter and being able to obtain a seat are different questions. Explaining that the first condition does not guarantee the second result does not deny the first condition's significance.

Do not rush to divide concepts into 'useful' and 'useless.' First identify whose position changes and with respect to which conduct. L1's permission to enter changes the registered member's position concerning entry. Whether seating is guaranteed depends on another condition.

<a id="ch1-confirmation"></a>
## 2. Successful confirmation is the condition establishing the seating duty

L1 then provides that only after the reservation system issues successful confirmation does the manager owe that reserving member a standard seat during the confirmed period. Both sides of the relationship are specified: the person receiving successful confirmation and the manager. The duty is to provide a standard seat during that period.

Accordingly, the reserving member's ability to require a seat from the manager follows from this independent provision of L1, not from 'this person may enter.' It requires a specific fact: the system has issued successful confirmation. Clicking to apply, seeing a queue notice, and receiving a 'received' message do not satisfy that condition. An application entering processing and a reservation receiving successful confirmation must not be conflated.

This also explains why the message's specific character matters. If the material says only 'a message was received,' do not assume it was successful confirmation. If successful confirmation is expressly stated, acknowledge that the condition is satisfied. The relationship depends on the fact required by the rule, not merely the person's subjective belief that the reservation is settled.

<a id="ch1-jia"></a>
## 3. A and D: the seats are equally full, but the grounds differ

Consider A first. A is only a registered member and demands that B yield after seeing all seats occupied. Registration gives A permission to enter the public reading area, but the supplied L1 contains no rule turning A's membership into B's duty to give way. A's membership alone therefore cannot establish that B must surrender the seat.

State this judgment's scope precisely: A's presently supplied grounds do not establish B's duty to yield. This is not an unsupported assertion that no one could ever owe such a duty in any circumstances. Only these rules are provided, and we cannot invent another basis for A's demanded duty.

<a id="ch1-ding"></a>
Now consider D. D has received successful confirmation, and the seats are also full on arrival. This time, besides permission to enter, the reservation relationship under L1 exists. The manager cannot deny the duty to provide D a standard seat during the confirmed period merely because 'everyone may enter.' Permission to enter has not replaced the duty created by successful confirmation.

Both A and D face the same fact of full occupancy, but the decisive conditions differ: A has only registration, whereas D also has successful confirmation. Moreover, A demands that B yield, while D's relationship concerns the manager's seating duty. The cases are not identical problems with names merely swapped. Identify separately whether a duty has a basis and who owes it. The manager's duty must not automatically be transferred to B, who currently occupies a seat.

These two relationships can coexist. A person's ability to enter and the manager's duty to provide that person a standard seat during the confirmed period do not exclude each other. The former does not automatically create the latter, and the latter does not disappear because the former also exists.

<a id="ch1-cable"></a>
## 4. Borrowing a charging cable: similar wording cannot expand a rule

The material includes a boundary example. An employee says 'you may borrow a cable,' but promises neither priority nor a duty to deliver a charging cable to a specific person. Hearing 'may borrow' does not authorize adding 'the employee must give it to me immediately' or 'I have priority in obtaining it.'

The connection to seating is that permissive language does not automatically establish another person's duty to provide something. The boundary is equally important: L1's provision of a standard seat after successful confirmation governs only the seating relationship. Both activities occurring in the society does not turn cable-borrowing permission into successful seat-reservation confirmation, nor turn the seating rule into a cable-delivery rule. Comparison helps test an inference; it does not create rules absent from the materials.

<a id="ch1-geng"></a>
## 5. Return to G: what can we establish now?

Return to G from the opening. As a member, G has permission under L1 to enter the public reading area. On Monday, G also received successful confirmation for Wednesday 10:00–12:00, satisfying the condition establishing the reservation relationship. Under L1, the manager consequently owes G a standard seat during that confirmed period.

We have now established the starting point for later analysis, but cannot treat it as the final outcome. After establishment, valid cancellation, suspension during a particular period, and expiration each require their corresponding rules and facts. Chapter 1 has not yet developed those rules, so the accurate conclusion now is 'the reservation relationship was established,' not that G must ultimately be able to remain seated until 12:00.

The next question is precisely how an established relationship changes. Carry forward the two objects distinguished here: the member's permission to enter, and the seat claim created by successful confirmation with the manager's corresponding duty. Distinguishing them is necessary to determine which relationship a later act changes.

<a id="ch-2"></a>
# Chapter 2: After formation—what do cancellation, suspension, and expiry each change?

Chapter 1 established G's reservation relationship. Now follow events through time: when the reservation holder asks to cancel or the venue develops a safety problem, do the original seat claim and seat-provision duty still exist? The answer depends on what happened, whether the rule's conditions are satisfied, and what effect the rule assigns to that event. We continue to use only this course's synthetic exercise rules.

<a id="ch2-withdrawal"></a>
## 1. Unilateral cancellation depends on receipt; later negotiation has separate conditions

L2 permits unilateral cancellation before 18:00 on the day preceding the reservation. The exercise's supplementary rule resolves whether the original phrase “before 18:00” includes exactly 18:00: it does. This course therefore consistently says “no later than 18:00.” Do not reinterpret the boundary as strictly earlier than 18:00 or generalize it into a rule of real-law interpretation.

Two conditions must be connected. First, the cancellation notice must fall within the deadline. Second, L2 makes cancellation effective upon receipt by the system. Thus compare receipt time with the deadline, rather than merely checking when the reservation holder clicked send. Sending means the notice left the sender; receipt means the system obtained it. Several minutes may separate those events, and those minutes may change the unilateral-cancellation result.

“Unilateral” means that, if these conditions are satisfied, L2's cancellation effect occurs without separate administrator consent. After the deadline, that route is unavailable. The material separately permits negotiation, requiring both parties' agreement. Sending a negotiation request shows only a wish to change the relationship; the other's agreement cannot be added to the facts. Failed unilateral cancellation means neither that separate negotiation is impossible nor that negotiation has succeeded. Check each route's own conditions.

<a id="ch2-arrival-comparison"></a>
## 2. E and F: why can a few minutes change the conclusion?

E sends notice at 17:50, and the system receives it at 18:05. Sending is before 18:00, but receipt is after it. L2 specifies receipt as the event needed for effectiveness, so this notice cannot extinguish the reservation through unilateral cancellation. Selecting only 17:50 would substitute a different fact for the one the rule requires.

F's notice arrives at 18:00. Under the exercise's supplementary rule, exactly 18:00 remains within the deadline and therefore takes effect in time. Other conditions being equal, the decisive comparison is receipt time: E's is after the deadline and F's exactly at its boundary. The supplementary rule including that boundary must also be preserved. Without that premise, this course's conclusion cannot simply be transferred.

<a id="ch2-withdrawal-scope"></a>
Effective cancellation extinguishes the seat claim and corresponding administrator's duty arising from that reservation. It does not cancel membership or L1 permission to enter the public reading area. This returns to chapter 1's two relationships: losing a particular reservation's seat claim does not deprive a member of entry permission. When analyzing legal effects, identify the particular relationship that changes rather than saying “all rights are gone.”

<a id="ch2-suspension"></a>
## 3. Safety closure causes suspension, not automatic cancellation

L3 concerns a different event: an immediate safety hazard requiring the venue to close. When those linked conditions occur, the administrator may suspend the seat-provision duty and must refund reservation points for the interval actually affected. The material does not define ordinary inconvenience, scarce seats, or the reservation holder's absence as such a safety closure.

Suspension and cancellation have different effects. Cancellation extinguishes that reservation's seat claim and corresponding duty; L3 closure does not automatically cancel it. After safety is restored within the original confirmed interval, the remaining time stays valid. Thus the same reservation remains the object of analysis before and after closure; only the interval actually affected cannot receive normal seat provision.

Reopening does not restart the clock. The originally confirmed end time remains unchanged. If the reservation ends at 12:00 and safety is restored at 11:00, the remainder is 11:00–12:00, not a new full original duration starting at 11:00. This distinguishes suspension from extension: L3 provides the former, not the latter.

<a id="ch2-xin"></a>
## 4. H's complete interval: how much remains after reopening?

H reserves 10:00–12:00. The venue closes for safety reasons from 10:00 to 11:00 and reopens at 11:00. The hour at 10:00–11:00 lies within the reservation and is affected by closure: the seat-provision duty is suspended during it, and that hour's reservation points must be refunded. The interval 11:00–12:00 remains within the original confirmation and stays valid after safety is restored, so H may use that hour.

At 12:00, the reservation expires at its originally confirmed end. Expiry means the scheduled interval has reached its endpoint. It differs both from extinguishing a reservation through timely cancellation and from suspending performance during safety closure within the reservation. Distinguishing all three explains why an hour remains after reopening at 11:00 and why original-reservation seat provision cannot still be demanded after 12:00.

Change one more fact: H arrives only at 11:30. The venue reopened at 11:00 and the original end remains 12:00, leaving half an hour after arrival. Personal lateness from 11:00 to 11:30 cannot be appended after 12:00 to extend the reservation to 12:30. Changing arrival time does not change L3's limit based on the original endpoint.

<a id="ch2-lateness-objection"></a>
A student in the synthetic material argues that anyone who has not sat for the full two hours should receive all points back. That objection treats actual seated time as the refund criterion. L3 instead uses the interval affected by safety closure. H's inability to use a seat during closure at 10:00–11:00 and H's own arrival only at 11:30 have different causes. The former triggers this refund rule; the latter does not become safety closure merely because H was not sitting in a seat. Thus actual use of only half an hour entails neither a refund for both hours nor adding the late half-hour to the closure-refund duration. This conclusion is limited to L3; no other lateness-refund rule has been invented.

<a id="ch2-geng"></a>
## 5. How does G's relationship change on Tuesday and Wednesday?

Return to G with these distinctions. The Wednesday 10:00–12:00 reservation was successfully confirmed. Under L2, unilateral-cancellation notice must reach the system no later than Tuesday 18:00. G sends the request Tuesday at 17:58, but the system receives it only at 18:02. Sending is at 17:58; receipt at 18:02 is the event this rule requires. Receipt is late, so effective unilateral cancellation cannot be established.

The alternative route is mutual negotiation, but the available facts provide no evidence that the administrator agreed. G's sending a cancellation request cannot replace mutual agreement. On the supplied facts, the reservation is therefore not extinguished by that request, and subsequent analysis starts with a valid Wednesday 10:00–12:00 reservation.

The venue closes for safety reasons Wednesday 10:00–11:00 and reopens at 11:00. Under L3, the seat-provision duty is suspended during 10:00–11:00, making that the affected interval requiring a points refund. The interval 11:00–12:00 remains valid and expires at the original 12:00 endpoint. Safety closure neither cancels the entire reservation nor moves the end to 13:00.

We have now established the relationship and intervals needed to calculate consequences. The next chapter applies the rate to determine the refund and independently judges review timeliness. Establishing a valid reservation before finding its overlap with closure prevents an arithmetically correct calculation with false premises from being mistaken for a correct answer.

<a id="ch-3"></a>
# Chapter 3: How many points must be refunded, and is review timely?

The first two chapters explained formation and change. Now apply those judgments to consequences and then to review when a dispute arises. The order matters: an effectively canceled reservation cannot still be treated as valid when calculating closure refunds. Even after the refund amount is known, application receipt must separately be checked against the review deadline. Substantive consequences and procedural conditions connect, but cannot substitute for one another.

<a id="ch3-calculation"></a>
## 1. Find the overlapping interval before using the rate

L4 charges 10 points per reserved hour and 5 per half-hour. This lesson addresses only whole half-hours; other increments are unspecified. For durations such as fifteen minutes outside the material's coverage, do not invent prorating, rounding, or a minimum half-hour charge. The supplied cases need none of those additional rules.

Combined with L3, the refund concerns overlap between safety closure and a valid reservation. Set out each interval's start and end, then identify the time during which a valid reservation exists and safety closure affects it. Closure before the reservation starts does not encroach on its valid interval and cannot count toward its refund.

Within the supplied increments, the calculation proceeds by first identifying overlapping duration, then converting at 10 points per hour or 5 per half-hour. The rate converts duration into points; it does not decide which duration belongs in the calculation. If the first judgment is wrong, even accurate multiplication afterward gives a wrong conclusion.

<a id="ch3-ren"></a>
## 2. I's demonstration: why does an hour of closure refund only half an hour?

I reserves 10:00–12:00; the venue closes 9:30–10:30. Closure totals one hour, but its first half-hour, 9:30–10:00, precedes I's reservation. Only 10:00–10:30 lies within both the valid reservation and safety closure. The actual affected duration is therefore half an hour, yielding a 5-point refund under L4.

Calculating from the entire one-hour closure yields a refund of 10 points. The error is not the product of “one hour times 10,” but counting 9:30–10:00 outside the reservation as I's lost reservation time. Both calculations use the same price; the difference is whether the applicability conditions are satisfied.

After reopening at 10:30, the original reservation has not automatically been canceled; 10:30–12:00 remains valid and expires at the original 12:00 endpoint. This also returns to chapter 2: establish that the relationship and original interval remain before considering the affected portion. Refunding half an hour does not invalidate the remaining hour and a half.

<a id="ch3-review"></a>
## 3. L5 review: examine timely receipt and substantive correctness separately

L5 requires members disputing a return of reservation points to seek review within seven days after learning of the decision. The count starts on the following day, and receipt by the system before the end of day 7 is timely. At least two temporal facts are needed: when the member learned of the decision and when the application reached the system. Clicking send cannot substitute for receipt.

The review body examines application of the rules and temporal facts. A prior administrator decision does not establish its own correctness. Review must reconsider whether confirmation, cancellation, closure, rates, and relevant times support that result. Likewise, receipt within the deadline establishes only procedural timeliness, not that every substantive applicant claim succeeds. The requested refund amount still requires stepwise judgment under L1–L4 and the corresponding facts.

The material also does not provide that seeking review automatically suspends other decisions. “Review has been requested” must not become “other decisions are no longer being implemented.” This course can explain L5's express review and timeliness conditions, but cannot derive an unspecified suspension effect from the mere existence of procedure.

<a id="ch3-gui"></a>
## 4. J's deadline: the day of knowledge is not day 1

J learns of the decision on Thursday. Under L5, counting starts the next day: Friday is day 1, Saturday day 2, Sunday day 3, the following Monday day 4, Tuesday day 5, Wednesday day 6, and Thursday day 7. Notice reaching the system before the end of that Thursday falls within day 7. The supplied fact of receipt “on the following Thursday” is therefore timely.

This method comes from the exercise's express text. It counts days and supplies no separate working-day rule. Including Saturday and Sunday follows the problem's seven consecutive days, not a declaration of deadline rules across jurisdictions. If we know only receipt on the following Thursday but not when the decision became known, we cannot locate days 1 and 7 or invent a judgment that the deadline expired or the application was timely. Retain the missing commencement fact explicitly.

<a id="ch3-geng"></a>
## 5. G's full analysis: what is wrong with debiting 20 and refunding 5?

Now connect G's experience from Monday through the following Wednesday.

First, formation. G is a member and receives successful confirmation Monday for Wednesday 10:00–12:00. Membership supplies entry permission; successful confirmation separately creates the administrator's duty to provide G a standard seat during that interval. Under L4, two reserved hours cost 20 points, so the debit of 20 matches the rate for the interval. That does not yet answer how many points should ultimately be retained.

Second, change. Tuesday 17:58 is only the cancellation request's sending time; receipt occurs at 18:02, after the preceding-day 18:00 boundary. Unilateral cancellation does not take effect. With no evidence of the administrator's agreement to negotiation, extinction by mutual agreement cannot be established either. Wednesday's analysis must therefore continue from a valid 10:00–12:00 reservation.

Third, the consequences of safety closure. The venue closes Wednesday 10:00–11:00 and reopens at 11:00. Closure overlaps the valid reservation by exactly one hour. L3 requires refunding that hour's points, and L4 gives an amount of 10. The interval 11:00–12:00 remains valid; the prior hour's closure neither cancels it automatically nor extends the end. Accordingly, 10 of the 20 points debited must be returned, leaving 10 under the supplied rules. Refunding only 5 leaves a shortfall of 5 and fails to apply this overlap and rate correctly. The correction is another refund of 5, bringing the total to 10. That conclusion jointly applies confirmation, cancellation, closure, and pricing; it is more than a “20 minus 5” arithmetic problem.

Finally, review is examined independently. G learns of the decision Thursday, applies the following Wednesday, and the application arrives that day. Under the daily count above, Wednesday is day 6, before the end of day 7, so the application is timely. G is also a member disputing a refund, fitting L5's supplied application situation. The substantive 5-point shortfall and procedural timeliness each have their own rules and facts. Timeliness does not imply that every refund demand should succeed; discovering a substantive error does not excuse checking the deadline.

This analysis does not replace G's receipt time with sending time, turn one hour of closure into cancellation of two hours, or assume the review application automatically suspended other decisions. Preserving each step's conditions produces a complete analysis.

<a id="ch3-variant"></a>
## 6. Change only receipt time: which conclusions must change?

Keep G's other facts and change only cancellation-notice receipt to Tuesday 17:59. Receipt is now no later than 18:00, so unilateral cancellation takes timely effect under L2. That reservation's seat claim and corresponding duty are extinguished; membership and entry permission do not disappear.

This directly changes the premise for later calculation. The original case could refund the 10:00–11:00 overlap because a valid Wednesday 10:00–12:00 reservation existed. In the variant, the reservation has been canceled, so it cannot still be treated as valid for overlap with Wednesday's closure and mechanically assigned the same 10-point refund.

This does not establish either “refund 0” or “timely cancellation necessarily refunds all 20.” L2 specifies the effect of timely cancellation on the relationship, but not points settlement afterward. L3 concerns safety-closure effects on a valid reservation, not cancellation settlement. The variant thus establishes extinction of the reservation without determining the final points balance. A unique amount requires an additional timely-cancellation settlement rule. Within the available material, retaining this gap marks where reasoning must stop. An answer that seems fair, convenient, or familiar cannot supply a missing premise.

<a id="ch3-exam"></a>
## 7. How should case answers be organized, and why need not every question have four parts?

The teacher's examination advice in the synthetic material is that complete cases may follow “formation—change—consequences—review.” G's case demonstrates this sequence: establish who owes what to whom, check cancellation or suspension, calculate the corresponding points, and finally examine review. Each section supplies necessary premises for the next, helping expose omissions.

The teacher also explains that conceptual-comparison questions need not mechanically use these four parts. If a question asks only to compare suspension and cancellation, explain their triggers, affected relationships, and effects after reopening; do not invent points facts or review dates. The answer's structure should serve the relationships the question asks about. This retains the synthetic course teacher's arrangement: explain why conditions affect results, without promising that any real examination will mark answers this way.

<a id="ch3-synthesis"></a>
## 8. Return to the course problem: connect four distinctions

G's experience makes the opening whole problem concrete. Entry permission tells us members may enter; successful confirmation creates a seat claim and corresponding administrator's duty for a particular interval. L2 and L3 explain extinction or suspension of that relationship; L4 converts the rule-selected affected interval into points; L5 supplies a procedure for reviewing the decision.

Looking back along this sequence, each of four distinctions prevents a specific error. First, sending differs from receipt, so sending at 17:58 cannot conceal late receipt at 18:02. Second, suspension differs from extinction, so the remaining hour stays valid after one hour of safety closure. Third, actual seated time differs from the affected interval, so personal lateness does not automatically enlarge a safety-closure points refund, and pre-reservation closure is excluded. Fourth, procedural timeliness differs from substantive correctness, so day-6 application receipt must still be combined with the rules to examine the points shortfall; it does not validate any amount automatically.

These distinctions supply no fixed answer detached from conditions. The original case requires a total refund of 10 points; with receipt changed to 17:59, settlement after timely cancellation is indeterminate because the rule is missing. Explaining when a conclusion holds, when it must change, and when the material is insufficient completes analysis of this exercise's system. All inferences remain within supplied synthetic rules. Reasons are developed where needed rather than replaced by “the previous lecture already proved it.”
