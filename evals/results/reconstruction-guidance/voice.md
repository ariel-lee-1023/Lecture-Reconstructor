# Who was at the gate?

Read these two sentences aloud. *Desde la cocina oí la verja. Dije que era mi hermana.* In English, “From the kitchen I heard the gate. I said it was my sister.” At what point does the sister enter the passage? Her first appearance is inside something the narrator says.

The first verb, *oí* (“I heard”), gives us a sound and a listener. From the kitchen, the narrator can hear the gate. The second verb, *dije* (“I said”), reports an act of speech. The words that follow tell us whom she identified. Our view stays in the kitchen throughout. We never get a second scene at the gate where we could see for ourselves who came in.

A student asked whether the narrator was lying if she had never seen her sister enter. Suppose she sincerely believed it was her sister. She could then be right about who came in, or she could be mistaken. Even if we later learned that her sister never came in, that discovery alone would tell us only that the identification was wrong. To call it a lie, we would need grounds for thinking she spoke against her own belief. *Dije* tells us what she said and leaves that belief open. The two sentences also leave open the possibility that her sister did enter.

To hear the difference one word makes, the teacher offered a variation of her own. In place of the original second sentence she put *Supe que era mi hermana*, translated here as “I knew it was my sister.” The same person identifies the same sister, but now the narrator claims knowledge. You can hear her greater certainty without gaining a second witness. You are still hearing her account from the kitchen.

The ending was left for the next class. We can already answer the student’s question from today’s two sentences. They give us good grounds for asking how the narrator identified her sister. They give us no grounds for accusing her of a lie.
