[Synthetic continuous three-chapter class; an interruption-and-resumption fixture. All rules, people, and data are teaching hypotheticals, not current law or an actual class. No preceding or subsequent class materials are available.]
Part one begins with the whole problem: How does the Study Society connect entry, reservations, cancellation, fees, and review into one system? Ultimately we must analyze G's full experience, not merely memorize five definitions. G is a member who reserves Wednesday 10:00–12:00 on Monday and receives successful confirmation. G later fails to cancel, the premises close on Wednesday, and G finally requests a refund of reservation points.
Exercise rule L1: Registered members have permission to enter the common reading area, but permission does not guarantee a seat. Only after the reservation system sends successful confirmation does the manager owe that person a duty to provide a standard seat during the confirmed period. Clicking an application button, a queue message, and an 'application received' message are not successful confirmation.
First consider A: A is merely registered and, finding all seats occupied, demands that B give up a seat. The rules do not derive B's duty to move from A's registration. Now consider D: D has successful confirmation, but all seats are occupied. The manager cannot deny the reservation duty by saying 'everyone may enter'; the two layers of relationship coexist.
An easily missed peripheral example concerns borrowing a charging cable. Staff say 'you may borrow a cable,' without promising priority or a duty to deliver to a particular person. The course rules address only seats; permission to borrow a cable must not be rewritten as seat confirmation.
A student asks whether permission is completely useless. It excludes a prohibition on entry under this rule, so it does have an effect. But that effect is not the same as ensuring all conditions are sufficient. Identify precisely what the concept concerns rather than forcing a binary choice between useful and useless.
This part returns to G: Successful confirmation is the starting point for subsequent relationships. We do not yet know whether cancellation, suspension, or expiry follows. For now, we can say only that the reservation relationship arose, not predict that G must ultimately be able to sit until 12:00.
Part two addresses changes to the relationship. L2: The person who reserved may cancel unilaterally before 18:00 on the preceding day; cancellation takes effect when notice reaches the system. Does receipt at exactly 18:00 count as 'before 18:00'? The exercise's supplementary provision expressly includes that instant, so this class consistently treats the deadline as no later than 18:00. After the deadline, negotiation is required, with both parties' agreement; sending a request is not agreement.
E sends notice at 17:50, but it arrives at 18:05 and cannot unilaterally terminate the reservation. F's notice arrives at 18:00 and takes effect in time. Only the receipt time differs; the other conditions are the same. Cancellation ends the seat claim and corresponding duty created by that reservation, not membership or permission to enter.
L3: When an immediate safety hazard requires closing the premises, the manager may suspend the duty to provide seats and must refund reservation points for the period actually affected. Closure does not automatically cancel the reservation. If safety is restored within the original confirmed period, the remaining period remains valid, without extending the end time.
Suppose H reserves 10:00–12:00; the premises close from 10:00 to 11:00 and reopen at 11:00. H may use the remaining hour and receives a refund of reservation points for the affected hour. Do not confuse suspension, cancellation, and expiry. The reservation expires at 12:00; even if H arrives only at 11:30, it is not extended by half an hour.
A student says that anyone who has not sat for the full two hours should receive all points back. The teacher responds: L3 calculates the period affected by safety closure, not the person's actual seated time. Personal lateness and the premises' inability to provide a seat have different causes.
G sends a cancellation request at 17:58 on Tuesday; the system receives it at 18:02, and there is no evidence of the manager's agreement to a negotiated cancellation. A safety closure lasts from 10:00 to 11:00 on Wednesday, with reopening at 11:00. Preserve these times; do not replace 'sent' with 'received' later. This part first determines whether the relationship still exists and which periods are suspended.
Part three turns to calculation and review. L4: Reservations cost 10 points per hour or 5 points per half-hour. This teaching exercise handles only whole half-hour increments; other increments are unspecified. L3 refunds are calculated from the overlap between closure and a valid reservation. Closure before the reservation starts does not count.
Complete demonstration: I has a reservation for 10:00–12:00, while closure lasts from 9:30 to 10:30. The overlap is half an hour, so the refund is 5 points. Incorrectly using the entire one-hour closure would yield 10; the error is counting time outside the reservation. The remainder from 10:30 to 12:00 remains valid.
L5: A member disputing a refund of reservation points must request review within seven days after learning the decision. Time starts on the following day; receipt by the system before the end of the seventh day is timely. The review body examines rule application and timing facts rather than presuming that the manager is always correct. The rules do not give a review request automatic suspensive effect over other decisions.
J learns the decision on Thursday; the following Thursday is day seven, and notice received by the system that day is timely. This method of counting days comes from the express exercise rule, not a general rule for all jurisdictions. If the date of learning the decision is missing, do not invent whether the deadline has expired.
G's subsequent facts are these: The system deducts 20 points but refunds only 5. G learns this on Thursday, requests review the following Wednesday, and the request arrives that day. Apply the facts already given—the confirmation in part one, failed cancellation and one-hour closure in part two—to decide whether the deduction and refund comply with the rules, then whether review is available. Do not calculate points while forgetting the procedural deadline, or infer that every substantive claim succeeds merely because the procedure is timely.
A small counterexample: If G's cancellation notice instead arrived at 17:59 on Tuesday, the reservation would have ended. The refund analysis based on 'overlap between a valid reservation and closure' could no longer be carried over. L2 does not specify how points are settled after timely cancellation. Record that variant's settlement as a gap in the materials; do not automatically apply L3.
The examination advice reflects this teacher's arrangements: Develop a complete case through 'formation of the relationship—change—consequences—review,' but do not mechanically write four paragraphs for a conceptual comparison. The teacher asks only for explanations of why conditions affect results and makes no promise that any real examination uses this scoring method.
At the end of the course, return to the original G. Readers should distinguish sending from receipt, suspension from termination, actual seated time from the affected period, and procedural timeliness from substantive correctness. All inferences are limited to the exercise rules above. No earlier lecture was supplied, so 'we proved this last time' cannot replace a necessary explanation.
