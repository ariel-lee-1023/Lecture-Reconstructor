# Should Archives Be Open? Rules, Interpretation, and Institutional Change

> Synthetic lecture on disagreement and institutional development: All institutions, materials, and views below were created for testing, not current rules, real publications, or real judgments. The course is set in 2019 and primarily discusses the 2018 exercise rules; the 2024 version is compared only as a later teaching version. No real-law verification or evidence of actual learner attitudes or outcomes is available.

Today we discuss a South Cape Archives exercise: Can researchers obtain ordinary archival records? I personally favor greater openness to facilitate the use of knowledge. Before analysis, however, put that preference in its proper place. A teacher's support for disclosure is an evaluation; whom a particular version permits to inspect records is a separate question requiring an answer grounded in its text.

This lecture also considers textbook interpretations, simulated judgments, and later rule amendments. All can inform our thinking, but each has a distinct identity and cannot substitute for the others in an argument.

## 1. Read the 2018 rule carefully first

Article 2 of the 2018 South Cape Archives Access Exercise Rules used in class provides:

> 'In principle, archival records are not provided to outsiders. Inspection shall be permitted if the applicant proves an academic research purpose and no sealed personal secrets are involved.'

This is a hypothetical classroom rule. It first establishes non-disclosure in principle, then requires inspection to be permitted when specified conditions hold. The exception requires both proof of an academic research purpose and the absence of sealed personal secrets in the records concerned.

An applicant's wish to inspect records is therefore insufficient by itself. Neither the research-purpose requirement nor the restriction concerning sealed personal secrets may be omitted.

Consider A and B. A submits an academic research plan, and the requested records contain no sealed personal secrets. B expresses curiosity without identifying an academic purpose. Both wanting inspection does not mean both qualify for the 2018 exception. A's case requires further interpretation of 'academic research'; B's facts do not establish a qualifying purpose, so curiosity alone cannot receive the same treatment as a qualifying research application.

## 2. Does 'academic research' require institutional funding?

The classroom textbook uses section 7 of the fictional 2018 Archival Institutions Exercise Notes, which treats 'academic research' as including only institutionally funded projects.

This is a restrictive interpretation: It makes institutional funding a threshold for academic research. But Article 2 itself states no funding condition, so the textbook's interpretation cannot become 'the rule expressly requires institutional funding.' The textbook author advances a claim about the provision's meaning, and that claim requires supporting reasons.

My view differs. I favor including independent research with a clear question and method, avoiding institutional status as a barrier to inspection. This approach focuses on the content and organization of research rather than whether the applicant receives institutional funding.

That too is an interpretive position, not the provision's express wording. Supporting it requires explaining why independent research can fit 'academic research,' why a research question and method can identify a research purpose, and how that understanding relates to the rule's purpose.

Comparing the interpretations allows different reasons and costs to be raised. Funding may provide a readily identifiable external indicator, but exclude research with clear questions and methods that lacks institutional support. Including independent research avoids a boundary based solely on institutional status, yet still requires deciding whether the applicant has supplied adequate evidence of a research purpose. These are arguments for comparison, not a claim that real-world data already show which standard works better.

Thus A's case cannot skip the interpretive dispute and simply assert that submitting a plan necessarily satisfies every interpretation. Under the teacher's interpretation, a clear question and method support classifying independent research as academic. Under the textbook's restrictive interpretation, institutional funding is additionally required. If the facts do not state whether funding exists, do not invent either its presence or definite absence.

## 3. A simulated judgment supplies one interpretive reason

The classroom mock-judgment booklet's summary of 'A's Application' states that the simulated panel regarded an independent researcher's research plan as sufficient proof of purpose, citing the aim of promoting the use of knowledge.

This material shows another approach: The simulated panel did not require institutional funding, instead assessing research purpose through the research plan and explaining that choice by reference to the use of knowledge.

But this remains a simulated judicial view, not a real court precedent. The summary gives no facts of other cases and does not claim binding force over them. We may assess its reasons' persuasiveness, but cannot infer that all applicants may inspect records in every circumstance or treat the summary as a basis for changing the provision.

Textbook interpretations, the teacher's position, and simulated judgments may agree or disagree. Each must nevertheless return to the 2018 provision being interpreted and explain the grounds of its conclusion.

## 4. Purposive interpretation cannot delete restrictions that remain

A student asks, 'If promoting the use of knowledge is the rule's purpose, could B's curiosity not also produce knowledge? Why should B be refused inspection?'

This question highlights the boundary between interpreting a rule's purpose and deleting its conditions. The 2018 version still requires an academic research purpose. Asking whether independent research is academic tests whether an activity fits the provision's concept. B merely expresses curiosity without identifying a research purpose, so proof of that condition is missing. 'Promoting the use of knowledge' cannot by itself remove the use restriction.

The same holds for personal secrets. C has a complete research proposal, but the requested records contain sealed personal secrets. Even if C satisfies the academic-purpose condition, the other condition fails. Both must hold; no degree of completeness in the plan cancels the presence of sealed personal secrets. C therefore cannot obtain inspection permission for the requested records through Article 2's 2018 exception.

Access to a copy with secrets removed may be worth discussing, but the course rules do not address it. A conceivable solution cannot be presented as a claim C already possesses, nor may the Archives' duty to provide such a copy be asserted.

## 5. What changed in the 2024 version?

The supplementary handout also contains Article 2 of the 2024 version:

> 'Ordinary archival records are in principle provided to outsiders; records involving sealed personal secrets must not be provided.'

First notice the time context. This course is set in 2019, and the main problem uses the 2018 rules. The 2024 text is a later teaching version, not a provision already effective in 2019. Having a later handout does not authorize silently replacing the version applicable to the original problem.

Placing the versions side by side reveals two connected changes. The 2018 version starts from non-disclosure in principle, then creates an exception for qualifying academic research. The 2024 version makes ordinary records available in principle and removes the academic-purpose threshold. The restriction concerning sealed personal secrets nevertheless remains.

B provides the clearest comparison. Under the 2018 version, B expresses curiosity without proving an academic purpose, so the research-inspection exception does not apply. Under the 2024 version, the lack of an academic research purpose no longer by itself prevents access to ordinary records. Whether those records contain sealed personal secrets must still be checked. Removing the academic-purpose threshold does not mean every record must be supplied.

Two questions must remain distinct. Asking 'How should an application be handled under the new version?' applies the 2024 rules. Asking 'Should the academic-purpose requirement be removed?' evaluates institutional design. The questions can inform one another but must not be conflated.

## 6. How to form your own comparative judgment

My evaluation is that the new version makes archives easier to use. But that evaluation cannot establish that general disclosure already existed under the 2018 version. Just as the textbook's funding criterion cannot masquerade as express text, a teacher's approval of later reform cannot change an earlier version's contents.

The assignment asks you to compare the two interpretations of 'academic research,' explaining their reasons and costs, rather than guessing the teacher's preferred conclusion. You may ask whether institutional funding is an appropriate indicator, why independent research with clear questions and methods should be included, and how each interpretation handles proof of purpose. Whichever you support, identify whether your reasons come from the provision, textbook interpretation, simulated judicial reasoning, or your own institutional evaluation.

Finally, test your wording against a common mistake: 'The teacher favors disclosure, so all records were public in 2018.' This substitutes an evaluation for a normative proposition and deletes the purpose and confidentiality conditions. Accurate expression must retain version, time, and the identity of each position: what the 2018 version provides, how different participants interpret it, what the 2024 version changes, and why you support or oppose that change.
