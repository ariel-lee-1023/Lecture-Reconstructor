# Editing spoken and quoted material without losing its meaning

**Format**: Three books | **Sections**: 9 | **Depth**: study  
**Sources**: Amy Einsohn and Marilyn Schwartz, *The Copyeditor’s Handbook*, fourth edition (2019), cited as ES; Carol Fisher Saller, *The Subversive Copy Editor* (2009), cited as Saller; and Ruth Edmonds Hill and Patricia Miller King, eds., *Guide to the Transcripts of the Black Women Oral History Project* (1991), cited as Hill–King. All page numbers below are printed pages in these editions.

## Mental Model

A spoken remark, a quotation from that remark, the document it refers to, and a later account of the event answer different questions. A speaker can explicitly correct a number. A publication can establish its own wording. A finding aid can identify a recording without supplying its words. The editorial decision turns on which of these objects is available and which assertion depends on it. A one-character change can require a substantive query. An entire disfluent sentence can sometimes be paraphrased accurately, provided the paraphrase is presented as such. [ES, pp. 52–54, 211–213; Hill–King, pp. ix–x, xv.]

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Identify the text whose wording matters

The author who uses a direct quotation bears responsibility for its accuracy. The copyeditor may spot a missing word, suspect a misattribution or identify an excerpt that distorts its source. A suspicious construction is a reason to examine the original or request a check. Fluent wording and repeated circulation do not establish authenticity. Careful editing of the manuscript and proofreading of a later typeset or converted version are separate operations: the latter can detect an error introduced during production, while a perfect rendering can reproduce an already inaccurate quotation. [ES, pp. 13–14, 211–212.]

Speech permits some changes of representation. A spoken honorific, ordinal or acronym can take its conventional written form. The handbook’s technical example represents the pronounced name of computer technology with the written acronym SCSI. A change from speech to conventional notation can preserve the term. Substituting a different term because it seems technically more convincing would require independent evidence about the identity of the term. In printed quotations, routine correction of a plain typographic mistake can be acceptable. When the spelling itself bears on a historical, literary or evidentiary point, it must remain visible or its normalization must be disclosed. [ES, pp. 211–213.]

A clean remark, an exact transcription and an independently checked statement of fact can therefore differ. The publication’s convention may allow deletion of voiced hesitation or slight repair of grammar. The permission ends where the cleanup changes what the speaker asserts or simply makes the speaker look better. When a tangle of clauses cannot be condensed without rewriting, an attributed paraphrase permits an economical statement of the point. Any words retained in quotation marks still have to meet the standard for quotations. [ES, pp. 212–213.]

### 2. Preserve who is speaking, what is reported, and where an extract begins

A speaker, the person whose words that speaker reports, and the author introducing the extract can coexist within one sentence. The placement of the reporting phrase and the extent of each quotation determine who is responsible for which words. Moving a passage from a quotation embedded in prose to a block ordinarily removes the outer quotation marks and changes the marks at the next level. Stripping every mark flattens the voices. On returning a block to running text, the outer and inner marks must again make clear which expressions belonged to the quoted speaker. [ES, pp. 214–219.]

A frame in indirect discourse can create a false change of person when it encloses a direct quotation. In the handbook’s interview example, the reporting sentence uses a third-person subject but the confession inside the quotation continues in the first person. A direct reporting frame restores the relationship without replacing the interviewee’s pronoun with an editor’s. Likewise a past-tense introduction can collide with a present-tense verb inside the excerpt. Recasting the lead-in or starting the quotation at a different, complete phrase can preserve both the grammar and the source. A quotation boundary that splits a verb from its necessary preposition can make an ostensibly exact excerpt harder to understand. [ES, pp. 220–221.]

A passage can be accurate at the level of individual words and still mislead when isolated. A pull quotation extracted from narrative may follow different house conventions from a direct quotation attributed to a third party. The latter keeps its speaker identifiable. Check the highlighted wording and its sense against the passage in the body, especially when abridgment removes a qualification. The mere presence of the full text elsewhere cannot tell a reader encountering only the extract that a different position was originally being qualified, challenged or reported. [ES, pp. 211, 217.]

### 3. Recover the evidential meaning of brackets and ellipses

Brackets can identify words supplied by the person quoting. Parentheses can be part of the document itself. An editor facing a phrase inside parentheses in a quotation must establish whether the phrase is in the source before changing its punctuation. A clarifying interpolation should be identifiable even if it makes the sentence much easier to read. An inserted grammatical ending can be marked; changing the surrounding sentence can avoid the interpolation altogether. In legal or literary texts where a small difference has interpretive significance, even adjusting the capitalization of the first quoted word may merit brackets. [ES, pp. 220–225.]

Dots inside quoted words have several possible histories. The source may contain a pause or an unfinished utterance. The person extracting it may have omitted words, or a translator may have made the cut. An editorial note can identify an ellipsis already present in the source. In a translation, bracketed ellipsis points can mark a cut made by the translator. The same visual appearance does not establish the same editorial action. Under one scholarly convention a period plus three dots indicates that a cut crossed a sentence boundary; a cut within one sentence takes three dots. Other house conventions exist. The evidence of an omission matters even when its typographic presentation varies. [ES, pp. 221–224.]

The handbook’s example from instructions to a tax form first limits an amount at the level of the taxpayer, then explains how it may be allocated among businesses. A shorter quotation jumps from the statement of a taxpayer-level limit to the possibility of allocation. The full wording explicitly rejects applying the limit separately to each business. That lost clause matters if the question is whether each business gets its own limit. The abbreviated quotation can support a description of allocation while giving a reader less evidence for resolving the separate-limit question. The example concerns how much of the original argument survives a cut; the historical tax illustration is not a statement of current law. [ES, p. 223.]

### 4. Decide whether an apparent error calls for repair, clarification or retention

The reader’s need identifies the cost of leaving something uncertain. A false start with a clear self-correction, a preference between ordinary spellings, a statistical inconsistency, and an unfamiliar term cannot all be settled by a general rule of consistency. When a word is intelligible but looks odd, ask what evidence makes it erroneous. In a printed quotation the available responses include reproducing the wording as found, identifying an original error where it genuinely matters, supplying a visible clarification, or replacing the quotation with a paraphrase. Repeated editorial markers on historical spellings can obscure the passage and convey contempt; a single statement of transcription practice may explain the whole class of readings. [ES, pp. 52–54, 211–213.]

A house convention about punctuation can reach an issue the convention cannot decide. In the handbook’s department-list example, supplying a serial comma can create a separate media department beside a print-advertising department. The list without the added comma can identify a single combined department. The fact that decides the punctuation is the organization’s actual structure. Where two places in a manuscript instead report different population figures, the useful query places both numbers and their locations together. A brief request for a defined piece of evidence gives the author something to resolve. An unexplained question mark or a general invitation to clarify shifts all of the diagnostic work back to the author. [ES, pp. 52–54.]

Technical expertise also has a boundary. In Saller’s Yorùbá example the editor sees different diacritics, word divisions and capitalizations in otherwise similar phrases. Presenting the actual variants identifies the problem without guessing which form is linguistically correct. Resolving them in the glossary first provides a basis for checking recurrence through the text. The decision may preserve intentional variation. A dictionary’s silence about a technical term can motivate a gloss for general readers while leaving the specialist’s justified choice intact. [Saller, pp. 24–26.]

A pattern of usage can answer whether a form occurs in a particular time, context or variety of English. It cannot, by its frequency alone, explain the sense of a particular occurrence. A disagreement between a corpus graph and an editor’s intuition can reflect the corpus’s composition or another sense of the search term. Inspecting examples in context is a necessary step before treating an apparent trend as a usage change. The proper question is narrower than whether the word appears anywhere. [ES, pp. 76–78.]

### 5. Resolve numbers at the level of the claim

A percentage, a percentage-point difference, a basis-point change and a percentile report different relations. In the handbook’s interest-rate illustration, movement from 5.50 percent to 6.00 percent is half a percentage point, or 50 basis points. The relative increase requires comparing the difference with the initial rate. A percentile instead locates a result among other results. Preserving all the digits while changing the unit or baseline can change the assertion completely. A high percentage of a small income stream can represent fewer dollars than a lower percentage of a larger stream. [ES, pp. 194–196.]

The ambiguity also arises when a sentence says a cost was underestimated by 93 percent. The actual cost could be 93 percent above the estimate, or the estimate could be 93 percent below the actual cost. Those percentages use different denominators and yield very different relationships between the amounts. State which quantity is measured against which once the quantities are known. If both readings fit the supplied information, arithmetic can demonstrate the difference but cannot select the intended reading. The same caution applies to expressions such as “times less”: a compact comparative expression can cost the reader more effort than an explicit ratio. [ES, pp. 208–210.]

### 6. Read the exhibit before using it to repair the prose

The first reading of a table belongs at the passage that invokes it. Identify the question the table is supposed to answer, whether it contains the evidence the text claims, and whether the two agree. A second pass checks the internal data, row and column identities, headers, notes and source. A later comparison across tables detects inconsistent units, formatting or definitions. These passes test different relationships. A well-formatted table can still contradict the text; correcting its number or heading without examining the underlying information could change the wrong object. [ES, pp. 247–253.]

In the handbook’s election example, 2,111 men and 1,904 women voted. Comparing those two totals does not answer how likely a registered member of either group was to vote. The relevant bases are 3,515 registered men and 3,344 registered women. The comparison is approximately 60 percent against 57 percent. A table of only nonvoters or only the two vote totals omits information needed to reconstruct the two rates. Conversely, where turnout is the point and every registered person is accounted for, a separate percentage column for nonvoters adds little. The meaning of a comparison determines which details are dispensable. [ES, pp. 250–251, tables 13–14.]

A line in a figure can also imply a proposition the observations did not establish. Quarterly rankings in the handbook’s fund example occur at discrete observation points, and a fund cannot occupy a fractional rank. Connecting the observations with lines suggests intermediate values through which the rank traveled. Distinct points preserve the measurements people actually made. A dual-axis figure requires each series to be read on its own scale, even where the curves appear close or cross. The resource to preserve is the labeled relationship between mark and quantity, not a verbal image of a shape detached from its legend. [ES, pp. 263–264, 269–270, figures 12–13.]

### 7. Separate testimony, indexing, revision and access

A retrospective oral account can supply an understanding of events and relationships that were left out of written records. It can also be partial, personal or imprecise. The Hill–King project selected 72 women to provide a cross section of contributions, without scientific sampling or a claim to include every significant individual. Individual recollection, corroboration of particular events and generalization to a population have different evidential requirements. The project offered each memoirist an opportunity to edit and correct the transcript before final copy. The offer establishes a process, rather than a finding that every statement was corrected or independently verified. [Hill–King, pp. ix–x.]

A finding aid supplies several distinct objects: a biography composed for the guide, a selective account of the interview’s topics, and information about the transcript and recording. Its subject entries are useful for discovery. The index is expressly an index to the guide. A missing entry cannot demonstrate that the subject never arose in an interview. A listed subject establishes neither a verbatim formulation nor where it occurred in the underlying exchange. Brackets in this guide mark approximate information. Some transcripts were still being processed when it was compiled. [Hill–King, p. xv.]

The entry on Anna Arnold Hedgeman illustrates the difference. It records four interview dates over two years and separate text and tape identifiers. It is marked incomplete and leaves the date of the copyright entry uncertain. The following pages contain a substantial biography and extensive lists of people, events and organizations. Their detail does not fill the missing date or convert the guide’s description into Hedgeman’s own wording. The historical entry also distinguishes temporary closure of the transcript, conditions that would apply to a loan after its release, conditions for quotation, and permission for the tape. A change in one of those conditions would not establish a change in all the others. Those arrangements applied when the guide was compiled. Present access requires the applicable current record. [Hill–King, pp. 44–46.]

### 8. Carry an editorial decision only as far as its evidence

A style sheet records choices, including justified exceptions. It need not inventory every stray space. If the reason for a change is that the original author consistently distinguished two specialized senses, turning both into one standard term would defeat the purpose of recording the decision. In Saller’s account of a scientific text, *causal* contact became *casual* contact. Both forms pass an ordinary spelling test; only one preserves the technical relationship. A search can locate all apparently similar occurrences, but the change itself needs a trial against their contexts. A visibly distinct test copy reduces the chance that the test version becomes the working manuscript. [ES, pp. 58–62; Saller, pp. 75–77.]

Authorial corrections and editorial proposals have different standing. Keep the original, edited copy, returned amendments and final text identifiable. When edits are made to two copies at once, a restoration of the original, rejection of a suggested change, and genuinely new material may become impossible to distinguish. Reconcile additions and deletions against the current working copy and reread neighboring text, numbered items, references and unanswered queries affected by a returned amendment. Preserve a recoverable record before clearing old markings from the final reading copy. [ES, pp. 66–68; Saller, pp. 46–48, 75–77.]

### 9. Choose work that changes what the reader can understand

An explanation of an edit is most informative when it identifies the actual obstruction. Saller’s examples include a person referred to before being introduced and a word initially read as a verb. These observations identify an error a reader could make and the location at which it becomes possible. The author can then decide whether introducing the person or changing the sentence is warranted. Faced with an unfamiliar discipline, an editor can defend the need for an intelligible passage while deferring on the technical solution. Rewriting everything into the editor’s preferred idiom obscures that division of responsibility. [Saller, pp. 24–26, 35–36.]

Time pressure favors deliberate triage over progressively lighter attention as the pages run out. A selective second pass can return to previously flagged uncertainties after a whole-text pass. Which defects deserve early attention depends on the work: a discrepancy that changes an interpretation or leaves the source of a figure uncertain carries a different cost from a harmless stylistic variation. When clarification is essential, give the author enough context to resolve the issue. The distinction between a question that can reasonably be answered “no” and a request for the missing definition makes a difference if the argument depends on that definition. [ES, pp. 26–28, 52–54.]

## Worked Example

**Which product did the speaker say would increase revenue?** In a spoken-quotation example, a speaker begins with product line 405, immediately corrects the identifier to 4055, and predicts a 10 percent revenue increase. The unedited record contains two identifiers. A reader trying to understand the commercial claim needs to know whether two introductions are being compared or whether the first number was abandoned. The evidence is inside the turn: the speaker signals a correction and gives a replacement. The prediction attaches to the second number. Listing both as product lines would manufacture a second causal claim. [ES, pp. 212–213.]

Under the publication convention in the example, removing the hesitation and superseded identifier yields a readable account of the prediction for line 4055. The modal force remains essential: an expected increase is not an increase already observed. The percentage also belongs to the forecast, independently of whether an editor regards it as plausible. The same passage supports two different treatments depending on the question. To report the substance of the forecast, use the corrected referent. When the utterance itself and its self-correction are the evidence under discussion, retain the sequence that makes the change intelligible. No comparison with a later sales figure could establish which identifier the speaker corrected. [ES, pp. 211–213.]

The next example concerns a speaker whose words are too tangled for minor cleanup to do useful work. A paraphrase can present the concern about technological tools displacing informed human judgment; a short, attributable fragment can preserve the distinctive words worth quoting. The two operations have visibly different boundaries. The scope of a direct quotation cannot expand simply because an editor can reconstruct a fluent version of the argument. [ES, p. 213.]

## Decision Rules & Judgment

- Preserve the wording of an odd but verified printed passage when its form is evidence. Show an interpolation, identify an original ellipsis, or use an attributed paraphrase when exact quotation would misrepresent what the available source supports. [ES, pp. 211–213, 220–225.]
- Resolve a minor mechanical ambiguity directly when the meaning is established. Query a one-character change if it would alter the number of entities, the speaker, the scope of a condition or the baseline of a comparison. Supply the concrete alternatives. [ES, pp. 52–54, 194–196, 208–210.]
- Compare the prose with the exhibit, then inspect the exhibit’s own units, labels, sampling and time points. Identify whether an apparent inconsistency concerns the source data, the figure or the interpretation. [ES, pp. 247–253, 263–270.]
- Preserve the distinction between an individual’s own account, later review of the transcript, a catalog description, and permission attached to a particular object and use. [Hill–King, pp. ix–x, xv, 44–46.]
- Where a disputed term recurs, collect actual variants and their contexts, settle the underlying distinction, then revisit the affected locations in the identifiable working copy. [Saller, pp. 24–26, 75–77; ES, pp. 58–62, 66–68.]

## Key Takeaways

1. A change in punctuation, speaker attribution, unit or quotation boundary can change the proposition while leaving most of its words intact.
2. An explicit self-correction can determine the intended referent. The truth of the resulting prediction or recollection remains a separate question.
3. A detailed catalog, a highlighted excerpt and a well-drawn chart each represent prior material selectively. Recover their provenance and limits before using them to support an inference.
4. Editing becomes accountable when the source, proposed change, evidential basis and affected passages remain distinguishable across revisions.
