# When does late notice cost a party its claim?

Two fictional contracts require notice. In one, the party that gave notice late lost its claim. A student asked why the other party should be treated any differently. To answer, read each clause twice. First ask what the party must do. Then look for words telling you what happens to the claim if the party fails to do it. For this exercise we have only the agreements and the fictional panel’s decision. We assume no background law.

<a id="north"></a>

On page 1 of the handout, North’s promise reads, “North shall notify East promptly after discovering a defect.” Discovery starts the clock. “Shall” tells us that North owes East notice, and “promptly” tells us when North must give it. Suppose North discovers a defect and delays long enough that its notice is no longer prompt. East can point to the promise and identify how North failed to perform it. Now look for the words that would make North’s claim lapse. This sentence is the whole agreement about notice, and it supplies no such consequence. From these terms alone we can identify a broken promise in that example and leave the fate of the claim open.

<a id="river"></a>

The student’s objection leads us to page 2. River’s clause says, “A claim lapses unless signed notice reaches the buyer by Friday at 17:00.” The opening words supply the consequence that North’s clause lacked. The word “reaches” picks the event to compare with the deadline. River’s courier left on Friday at 16:50, ten minutes before the cutoff. At that moment we know the notice was on its way. The buyer received it on Monday at 09:00. Only that second event satisfies the description “reaches the buyer,” and it occurred after the deadline. Under this clause, River’s claim lapsed.

<a id="signature"></a>

Notice what the panel actually decided on page 3. The parties disputed whether the name typed at the foot of the notice counted as a signature. Even if it did, a signed notice received on Monday would still have missed Friday’s cutoff. Late receipt therefore disposed of the claim on its own. The panel expressly left the signature question open. Its decision gives us no answer to what would happen to a timely notice bearing the same typed name.

<a id="drafting"></a>

The teacher observed that drafters often require receipt and said they would negotiate a fallback if the address changed. That is a reason to raise the address in negotiations. A preference expressed in class adds no fallback to River’s agreed wording. The answer to the student’s question lies in the two clauses themselves. East can point to North’s promise to give it prompt notice of a defect. River’s buyer can also point to the words that make timely receipt necessary to keep the claim alive.
