# Reading *The Envelope* twice

The class proposed that the empty envelope proves the letter was gone before Iris ever arrived. Read the story once with that idea in mind. Then go back and look for the moment when you actually saw inside the envelope. This lecture takes the complete story as its basis for testing the classroom reading.

> At noon Iris said the envelope felt light.
>
> At dusk Tomas opened it. He laid its empty halves on the table.
>
> “I saw what she found at noon,” he told us years later.

The narrator adds a footnote.

> I was with Tomas at dusk. At noon I was across the river.

At noon we have the word “light.” By dusk we have the word “empty.” The second word offers such a neat explanation for the first that we may remember them as if they meant the same thing. Try reading the opening again before accepting that explanation. Iris *said* something about how the envelope *felt*. The sentence gives us her impression of its weight. It gives us no view of its contents. The next sentence does. Tomas opens the envelope, and for a moment the story holds still as two empty halves lie on a table. This is the first scene in which the reader can place the emptiness at a particular time.

The classroom reading has an attraction. Imagine that the envelope was empty throughout. Iris’s remark would fit neatly with what Tomas later lays on the table. But imagine instead that a thin letter was still inside at noon and was removed before dusk. The two scenes would read just as they do now. The story says nothing about who sealed the envelope or where it was kept in the interval. Even proof that it was empty *at* noon would leave another gap in the claim that a letter disappeared *before* Iris arrived. For that, we would need some account of what had once been inside and when it went missing. A letter is so easy to supply in our imagination that we can miss that the lecturer was the first to name it.

Now listen to Tomas tell it. “I saw what she found at noon” gives Iris’s impression a new verb, *found*. His “I saw” lends it the weight of an eyewitness, and only after the quotation comes “years later.” **When does Tomas say he saw it?** “At noon” can go with “she found.” On that reading, he could be identifying what he saw at dusk with what he believes she found earlier. We can also hear him as claiming to have witnessed her discovery at noon. The story gives no independent account placing him beside Iris then. A later recollection is compatible with a genuine memory. The distance of years gives us reason to examine his wording and no grounds to accuse him of lying.

The two occurrences of “I” belong to different people. Tomas speaks the “I” inside the quotation. The “I” in the footnote belongs to the narrator, one of the “us” to whom Tomas spoke. “I was with Tomas at dusk” gives the narrator firsthand access to the scene at the table. “At noon I was across the river” tells us why the opening account of Iris cannot have the same eyewitness basis. It tells us where the narrator was, while leaving Tomas’s whereabouts at noon open. Placed last and off to the side, the footnote sends us back to a first sentence we may already have accepted in the same voice and with the same confidence as the second.

There is a way to earn the class’s theme of trust. The narrator earns a measure of trust by volunteering a limit on what they personally witnessed. We can accept the account of the empty halves, believe that Tomas later spoke those words, and still wonder whether his recollection reaches all the way back to noon. Regret is a more tentative reading. Returning to the incident years later may make it sound unfinished for Tomas, but he never says he wishes he had acted differently. Those degrees of confidence are part of the emotional experience of reading the story.

The lecturer asked you to remember the title for the workshop. It is *The Envelope*. The title names the object we follow from scene to scene. Iris can feel it, Tomas can open it, and years later he can speak about it. The reader who calls it “the missing letter” has already supplied an object and an event. The story’s achievement is to let us feel how readily we made that addition, then give us a reason to read again.
