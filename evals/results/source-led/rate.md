# What does “up ten” mean?

The student had a good reason for getting ten. The printed card records 30 failed inspections in the first 100 returns and 40 in the next 100. Subtract the two counts and you get ten more failed inspections. The question is what happens to that ten when you use it to describe a change in the *rate*.

Start with the lecturer’s observation that the two denominators are the same. In each period, dividing the number of failed inspections by 100 returns gives the share of returns that failed inspection. The first share is 30% and the second is 40%. On a percentage scale, the distance from 30 to 40 is **10 percentage points**. That is what the “ten” the lecturer reported writing in the margin can express.

The memo on the card also asks how large the increase was *relative to the old rate*. Now the reference quantity changes. Compare the ten additional failures per hundred returns with the original thirty failures per hundred returns. Ten is one-third of thirty, so the relative increase is **33⅓%**. In symbols, (40% − 30%) / 30% = ⅓. You can check the lecturer’s phrase “rose ten percent” by working backward. A 10% increase on 30% would add one tenth of 30 percentage points, or 3 points, bringing the rate to 33%. The card gives 40%. The transcript records the instruction as “Take the dif... difference.” Subtracting the two rates does give ten percentage points. The error was calling the result a ten percent increase.

There is one more denominator to inspect. What went into each hundred? The note on the card says the form counts every return, including repeat returns. Suppose, as a teaching example, one machine came back twice and failed inspection both times. It would contribute two failed inspections even though only one machine was involved. Two different machines failing once each would contribute the same two failures. The retained form has no machine IDs to distinguish these histories. The machine-generated recap’s claim about counting every distinct machine conflicts with that note.

The share of recorded returns that failed inspection went from 30% to 40%, an increase of 10 percentage points, or one-third relative to its starting rate. These records cannot tell us how many distinct machines failed, whether the same machines failed again, or whether an individual machine became more likely to fail.
