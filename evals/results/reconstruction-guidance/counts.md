# Museum Methods, Week 4 — Did people enter faster on poster Saturday?

A museum puts up a poster. On Saturday, its door counter records twenty more entries than it did the Saturday before. A student asks, “But we had twenty more entries. How can you say the pace didn't pick up?” The student has the difference exactly right. To answer the question about pace, we also need to know how long the counter was observed each day.

Start with what the instrument counted. Both totals came from the same door. A staff member who left and came back could register another entry. The numbers therefore tell us how many entries occurred at that door during each observation period. The number of different people who visited remains unknown.

| Observation | Entries at the door | Time observed | Average entries per hour |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Saturday before the poster | 60 | 3 hours | 60 ÷ 3 = 20 |
| Saturday with the poster | 80 | 4 hours | 80 ÷ 4 = 20 |

The figure of 80 is the count given in class. The working slide lists 90 and expressly marks that figure for replacement with the classroom count.

Now return to the student's twenty extra entries. At an average of 20 entries an hour, three hours yield 60 entries and four hours yield 80. The larger total is exactly what the same average pace gives over the longer observation period. This calculation leaves open how many entries actually occurred in the fourth hour. Each average spreads the entire count evenly over the hours for comparison. The arrivals themselves could have been uneven.

That distinction matters if someone asks whether the door was busier **at noon**. A count accumulated over three or four hours loses the timing of individual entries. The lecturer pointed to a dip in the lower line of the classroom chart but could not recall its time. The chart is missing from the course export. Locating the dip would require the chart with its time labels, and comparing the two Saturdays at noon would require counts for comparable intervals around noon. The two whole-period averages alone settle neither question.

For the memo, there is one further question. What would have happened on the second Saturday if the museum had left the poster down? The museum observed that Saturday with the poster and had no no-poster comparison for it. The first Saturday supplies a figure for a different day. Equal observed averages therefore leave the effect of the poster open.

A concise memo could say, “At the monitored door, entries rose from 60 in three hours before the poster to 80 in four hours with it. Both observation periods averaged 20 entries per hour. These counts include possible repeat entries, and the comparison does not establish whether the poster changed the pace of entry.”
