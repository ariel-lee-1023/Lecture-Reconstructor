# Legal reasoning in a lecture

**Format**: Six books and one law journal article (PDF) | **Sections**: 8 | **Depth**: study  
**Source**: The references below cite the original works by Anderson, Schum and Twining (2005), Duxbury (2008), Hohfeld (1913), MacCormick (2005), Prakken (1997), Toulmin (updated edition, 2003), and Walton, Reed and Macagno (2008). Page numbers follow the printed editions. Historical illustrations describe the questions and arguments in those works.

## Mental Model

A legal conclusion can fail at several different places. The event might be unproved. Everyone might agree on the event while disagreeing about the rule that gives it legal significance. Even a well-founded finding of breach can leave open which decision-maker may grant which remedy. Locating the disagreement determines what could resolve it. A finding about timing calls for different support from a holding about the effect of lateness. [MacCormick, chs. 3–4, pp. 32–77; Hohfeld, pp. 25–32.]

The position from which a question is asked changes what the materials can establish. An investigator can reasonably follow a lead whose strength would be insufficient for a verdict. A court can decide a point on an assumed premise without finding the event occurred. A proposition on which judges agree can also differ from the proposition that explains their agreement in the result. Keep the stage, speaker, identified issue, and permitted conclusion together. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, ch. 8, pp. 224–225, 236–244. Duxbury, ch. 3, pp. 67–73.]

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. Locate the disputed premise

A legal syllogism links a rule to facts satisfying its conditions and draws the consequence the rule specifies. Its usefulness is diagnostic. If the rule confers discretion to award compensation, establishing every condition for eligibility still leaves the exercise and amount of that discretion to be justified. In the historical *Dignity Funerals Ltd v Bruce* illustration, the question whether dismissal was one cause of the employee's wage loss could be answered before deciding what compensation was just and equitable. The first answer allowed the second question to arise. It did not fix a sum. [Neil MacCormick, *Rhetoric and the Rule of Law* (2005), ch. 3, pp. 32–48, particularly pp. 32–33.]

A dispute about **proof** concerns whether a factual premise is established. A dispute about **interpretation** concerns the meaning of a legal source. **Classification** asks whether the circumstances fall within a legally defined category. **Relevancy**, in this account, asks whether the proposed legal ground can justify this result. A dispute over “loss” can concern the meaning of the term, whether an expense was incurred, or whether the established expense falls within that meaning. Proof that an expenditure occurred can settle the factual question while the meaning of the governing provision remains open. [MacCormick, ch. 4, pp. 49–77, and chs. 7–8, pp. 121–162.]

The identity of the claimant also matters. In *Daniels v White and Tarbard*, as discussed in the source, the purchaser's contract with the seller and the alleged negligence of the manufacturer supplied different routes. The couple suffered injury from lemonade containing carbolic acid. The manufacturers were found free of culpable carelessness in production and bottling. The publican who sold the goods nevertheless owed the purchaser the obligation governing merchantable quality in that sale. His wife suffered a similar injury but was outside that contractual relationship. The point of the comparison is the difference in parties and conditions attached to the claims. Present product-liability rules are a separate inquiry. [MacCormick, ch. 4, pp. 49–50.]

### 2. Explain the warrant and its limits

**Grounds** supply the starting point for a claim. A **warrant** licenses the move from grounds of this kind to a conclusion of this kind. **Backing** answers the further question of why that warrant may be relied upon. The **qualifier** expresses the force the warrant gives the conclusion. **Rebuttal conditions** identify circumstances that would defeat this use. A new piece of information belongs wherever it does work: it may establish a ground, support a disputed warrant, or remove a particular exception. Evidence of an event and authority for its legal effect answer different objections. [Stephen E. Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*, updated ed. (2003), Essay III, pp. 87–100.]

Harry's Bermuda birthplace is the datum in Toulmin's historical nationality illustration. The inference to British nationality is presumptive under the schematic rule in the example. The relevant legislation backs that rule. A question about Harry's parents or a later change of nationality can affect its application even when birthplace is beyond dispute. Better evidence about the birth cannot determine which nationality law applied at the relevant time. The example concerns the roles within an argument and supplies no current citizenship rule. [Toulmin, Essay III, pp. 93–98.]

An argument using an acknowledged warrant differs from an argument trying to establish a warrant. Writing a conditional that repeats the desired conclusion can secure a formally neat inference without giving a reason to accept the conditional. A further instance that already presupposes that bridge has the same defect. Several cases in which the grounds and outcomes have each been checked independently can instead bear on whether a new warrant holds. An implicit premise can often be reconstructed from wording and context. When several premises could explain the passage, the reconstruction remains attributable to the analyst. Strengthening the speaker's position with a new reason is a further operation. Neither the logical position of an exception nor its place in a diagram assigns a legal burden to a party. [Toulmin, Essay III, pp. 89–114, especially pp. 111–112. Walton, Reed & Macagno, ch. 6, pp. 189–219.]

### 3. Separate a legal relation from the facts that establish or change it

A claim and its correlative duty identify the required conduct from the two parties' perspectives. A privilege to do a specified act means the holder owes the counterpart no duty to abstain from that act. The holder might separately owe a duty to perform it. A power concerns the ability to change a legal relation through an effective act. The counterpart's liability is exposure to that legal change. An immunity from a specified person's attempted change corresponds to that person's disability to effect it. Statements that appear opposed can both hold if the counterpart, conduct, time, or contemplated change differs. [Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld, “Some Fundamental Legal Conceptions as Applied in Judicial Reasoning,” 23 Yale Law Journal 16–59 (1913), pp. 28–59.]

A permission therefore leaves open whether anyone else owes assistance or noninterference. A separate undertaking may supply that duty. Conversely, a duty to refrain from a transaction may coexist with the power to carry it out effectively. In Hohfeld's conveyance illustration, an owner may retain the power to convey to Z despite having promised to refrain. An actual conveyance then raises two questions about different relations: its effect on title and its breach of a duty to the promisee. Finding the duty alone gives no answer on title or remedy. [Hohfeld, pp. 30–44, 44–55.]

Timing can distinguish the event that **creates a power** from the act that **exercises it**. In the historical right-of-entry analysis, breach of a condition can make the estate susceptible to divestment by a further act. The earlier event need not itself divest the holder. A transaction's constitution and its proof can likewise involve the same object at different times. Execution and delivery of an instrument in January may be operative facts. Inspecting the paper later supplies evidence about that transaction, with evidential questions about wording and delivery still possible. Where legal effect depends on absence of prior revocation, that negative condition and the procedural burden concerning it remain distinct. [Hohfeld, pp. 25–28, 44–55.]

**Offer or option: what can the parties change before acceptance?** An ordinary contractual offer places the offeree in a position to create a contractual obligation by an effective acceptance. The offeror is liable to that change. A power to revoke, where it exists, lets the offeror extinguish the offeree's power before acceptance. Consider what an agreement to keep the offer open could mean. On one view it creates a claim against revocation: revoking can breach the undertaking while still destroying the opportunity to accept. On the competing view of a legally effective option, the offeree retains a power of acceptance throughout the specified period and the option-giver lacks power to end it by attempted revocation. Under the second reading a later, otherwise qualifying acceptance during that period can create the obligation itself. Damages for an earlier breach are a different consequence. Distinguishing the two readings requires knowing what transaction was constituted, on what terms and conditions. The physical ability to communicate a withdrawal is common to both readings and settles neither. This is the article's historical analytical dispute over options supported by consideration or made under seal. [Hohfeld, pp. 49–53.]

### 4. Establish the facts the legal rule makes material

The **ultimate probandum** is the factual proposition, sometimes a compound proposition, the legal theory requires. Its material components are **penultimate probanda**. An **interim probandum** connects an evidential item to one of those components. Working down from the governing rule identifies what needs proof. Working up from the actual reports and exhibits identifies what the record can supply. A dispute over the rule can change which factual propositions matter under competing interpretations. [Terence Anderson, David Schum & William Twining, *Analysis of Evidence*, 2nd ed. (2005), chs. 2, 5, pp. 46–77, 123–144.]

An item can be relevant while leaving its credibility or weight doubtful. A witness's report is evidence of an event. Whether the event occurred depends in part on opportunity to perceive, accuracy of recall, how the belief was formed, and faithfulness in reporting that belief. Poor light can weaken an identification without impugning honesty. A recorded statement can also be offered for different purposes: its truth, notice to the listener, or evidence of the speaker's mental state. Each use needs its own path to a material fact and may raise a different admissibility question. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 2, 8, 11, pp. 46–77, 224–245, 289–314.]

The shape of support matters. Where a rule requires both A and B, strong evidence of A alone leaves B open. A chain carries uncertainty through successive links. Distinct circumstantial facts can converge on one proposition. Independent testimony about the same proposition may corroborate it, but three accounts derived from one observer do not provide three independent observations. Even the same independently reported error can arise under shared viewing conditions. These are reasons to trace dependence before combining evidence. Logical distinctness does not establish statistical independence, and an evidential assessment need not invent numerical probabilities. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 3, 9, pp. 78–111, 246–261.]

A denial, an explanation consistent with the evidence, and an affirmative rival account place different demands on a proponent. A rival story earns weight from the evidence for its material parts. An unsupported narrative may still identify a gap in the proponent's story without proving that the rival occurred. At the decision stage the burden bearer, issue, standard, and procedural posture matter. Failure to discharge a burden of proof can determine an issue without establishing the contrary historical event. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 3, 8, 10, pp. 78–111, 224–245, 262–288.]

**One letter, several possible uses.** In the historical Bywaters and Thompson material, letters were used to reason about the relationship of the parties, proposals described in the correspondence, and participation in the charged act. These propositions sit at different distances from the document. An authenticated letter can establish that certain words were written. Its wording and date may support an inference about the writer's attitude at that time. A statement describing conduct can still be inaccurate or fanciful. A proposed act can remain a proposal. During examination, Edith Thompson was asked what she had meant by the possibility of taking drastic measures. She testified that she meant leaving England with Bywaters. The phrase bears on the competing interpretations. Her later account is evidence for one interpretation and has its own credibility question. Treating the letter alone as a confession, or treating her explanation as conclusively true, would suppress precisely the inference in dispute. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, ch. 7, p. 211.] Even a hostile attitude does not by itself establish participation on the occasion charged. Each move towards the last proposition needs some reason why the evidence favors that reading over the alternatives. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, ch. 7, pp. 159–223.]

| Decision being made | What that evidence can do at this stage |
|---|---|
| Should an investigator pursue the account? | A specific described act can suggest a fact to check. The investigation can seek evidence that distinguishes a proposal from an act carried out. An unresolved alternative explains why another inquiry is worth making. |
| For what purpose is the letter offered? | Showing the expression of a particular attitude and proving the truth of a described event use the same words for different propositions. The court needs the purpose and the inferential connection before the governing admissibility rules can be applied. |
| Has the material factual issue been proved? | Identify the participant, act, time, and any required mental state under the applicable legal theory. Compare the evidence for each disputed step with the rival account, then apply the burden and standard for that issue. Proof of authorship cannot supply the missing link to participation. |
| What would contrary evidence change? | Material that discredits the account of an event weakens the inference that the event occurred. It can leave intact the fact that the writer expressed the idea. A remaining gap can defeat the party bearing the burden on participation without establishing every detail of the other side's account. |

The distinction between stages prevents a lead from becoming a finding merely by retelling it. It also prevents a favorable finding on one issue from deciding admissibility, a different issue, or a later remedy. The letters illustrate these inferential limits. A verdict on the historical record would require the rest of the evidence and the applicable law. [Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 7–8, 10–11, pp. 159–245, 262–314.]

### 5. Explain competing readings and institutional limits

Linguistic reasoning identifies the relevant ordinary or technical sense in context. Systemic reasoning reads a provision against related provisions, decisions, principles, and the legal structure it occupies. Teleological and evaluative reasoning considers the end or value served by a proposed construction. An enacted definition can displace an ordinary usage. An attempted harmonization can erase an express distinction. A purpose can explain why one reading fits the enactment, while leaving a proposed extension outside its terms. Objective purpose and the subjective intentions of particular lawmakers are different matters. The legal system determines the admissible materials and their weight. [MacCormick, ch. 7, pp. 121–142.]

A candidate ruling may be internally consistent yet arbitrary. **Consistency** tests for contradiction. **Coherence** asks what principle or value makes the legally relevant pattern intelligible. **Universalizability** asks whether the stated reason would be available in relevantly similar cases, including cases where the preferred party changes. This differs from predicting what most courts will do. A genuine counterexample can narrow the asserted reason when the limiting distinction has a legal justification. A different party name alone seldom supplies one. [MacCormick, chs. 5, 10, pp. 78–100, 189–212.]

A consequence can be juridical or empirical. The former concerns what else a proposed ruling would authorize or require. The latter predicts how actors or events will respond and needs a causal basis. A concern for reliance or fairness may be relevant within an existing standard, justify a choice entrusted to an institution, or support changing the law. Its role depends on the source and the decision-maker's competence. A prediction about desirable results cannot confer an absent power. An adverse judgment may govern the parties while its rationale remains contestable. A claim of error needs a separate basis for appeal or authorized departure. [MacCormick, chs. 6, 12–13, pp. 101–120, 237–280]

### 6. Read a judgment for its grounds, reach, and force

What was decided, why it was decided, and what force it has for another court are separate questions. A candidate **ratio decidendi** connects a proposition on a contested issue with the material facts, the reasoning, and the disposition. A previous court's persuasive analogy can travel farther than its authority, while its institutional position may constrain a later court that disagrees with its reasoning. Jurisdiction, hierarchy, procedural posture, period, and later treatment affect those judgments. If only an account of a decision is available, the grounds can be attributed only to the extent the account identifies them. [Neil Duxbury, *The Nature and Authority of Precedent* (2008), chs. 1, 3, pp. 1–30, 58–110; MacCormick, ch. 8, pp. 143–162.]

Inverting a proposed proposition and asking whether the outcome would change can reveal excessive generality. It can also mislead. When the court actually decides two independent sufficient grounds, deleting either alone leaves the result intact. That does not make both mere dicta. An argument the court expressly reserves is different again: its availability to the parties does not establish judicial endorsement. Where judges agree in the order but disagree about reasons, agreement has to be traced issue by issue. The narrowest imaginable opinion need not supply an authoritative common rationale. Considered dicta may still have substantial weight. [Duxbury, ch. 3, especially pp. 67–92; MacCormick, ch. 8, pp. 143–162.]

A distinction needs a difference that matters to the legal reason. A narrower formulation supported by that difference should preserve the earlier disposition on its own facts. Otherwise the apparent distinction may amount to repudiation. Overruling changes the earlier proposition's authority for subsequent cases. Reversal changes the disposition within the same litigation. The institutional power required for each is a separate question from the merits of correction. This analysis is historically situated and supplies no universal rule of binding force for all jurisdictions. [Duxbury, chs. 3–4, pp. 67–92, 111–149.]

**An order with a majority but no shared reason: Central Asbestos Co. Ltd v Dodd.** The claimant developed asbestosis as a result of the defendant's negligence. The House of Lords held, three votes to two, that his damages action was within time. The disagreement concerned when time began to run and when the claimant learned the relevant facts. Separating the questions gives the following alignment:

| Judicial position | What mattered to the start of time | Result for this claimant |
|---|---|---|
| Lords Reid and Morris of Borth-y-Gest | Discovering that he had a worthwhile cause of action. | In time. |
| Lords Salmon and Simon of Glaisdale | Knowledge of the facts constituting the alleged negligence. On their account he already knew these facts at the earlier date. | Out of time. |
| Lord Pearson | Ignorance of having a worthwhile cause of action did not postpone the period. On his view the claimant learned of the alleged negligence within the relevant period. | In time. |

The vote for the order comprises Reid, Morris and Pearson. The grouping on the legal significance of ignorance comprises Pearson, Salmon and Simon. Calling either group “the majority” without saying on what question makes it appear that three judges adopted one rationale to reach one result. The mistake makes a practical difference in *In re Harper and others v National Coal Board (Intended Action)*. There the plaintiff knew the material facts before the period but discovered the cause of action within it. The first-instance court assembled the three votes on knowledge of facts and refused leave. The Court of Appeal reversed. It found no discernible ratio common to the three who had reached the majority disposition in *Dodd*. A later reader can report the earlier result and each legal view without manufacturing a shared rule. [Neil Duxbury, *The Nature and Authority of Precedent*, ch. 3, pp. 69–73, especially pp. 71–72, notes 54–57.]

Multiple sufficient grounds in one judgment pose a different problem. If either ground suffices, removing one leaves the outcome intact. A purely mechanical necessity test then wrongly suggests that neither reason was part of the decision. The historical statements in *Jacobs v London County Council* and *London Jewellers Ltd v Attenborough* address the status of such reasons. The authority of two reasons given for the same judgment is a different question from whether separate judges ever endorsed the same reason. [Duxbury, pp. 72–73.]

### 7. Identify what an objection attacks

A question about a witness's position to know, an expert's field, or the force of an analogy targets different premises. An expert opinion depends on appropriate expertise, the statement actually made within that field, and its evidential basis. A contrary expert opinion can require comparison of the reasons offered by both. Counting credentials cannot resolve a disagreement about their common underlying data. An analogy depends on why similar treatment in the reference case should extend to this case. A competing comparison can expose the unspoken reason for selecting the original similarity. [Douglas Walton, Chris Reed & Fabrizio Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes* (2008), chs. 2–3, pp. 43–120, and ch. 9, pp. 308–346.]

A challenge may request support, contest the warrant, raise an exception, or supply an opposing argument. The effect of merely asking depends on the kind of premise and the dialogue's burdens. A possible alternative cause can expose an unsupported causal inference without establishing that alternative as fact. In an argument from lack of evidence, the missing item has probative value insofar as an adequately conducted inquiry could ordinarily be expected to find it. Once an objection has received an adequate answer on its own terms, repeating it adds no new rebuttal. [Walton, Reed & Macagno, chs. 1, 5, 7, 9, pp. 1–42, 163–188, 220–274, 308–346, particularly schemes 26–32]

**An analogical chain in which the proposed rule changes.** In the search example, a police officer can see something already exposed in a public place, open a bag to see its contents, or listen covertly to a conversation in a private place. The first is treated as outside the category of search in the book's comparison, while the other two fall inside. Now consider the dog that signals drugs in luggage in public without taking the bag or interfering with its use. The candidate explanation is that access to information in public without intrusion on a person, possession, or use falls outside the category. On its stated premises the dog example fits. That fit settles only the application of the proposed criterion. Its adequacy across cases remains open. [Walton, Reed & Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes*, ch. 2, pp. 45–47, 75–76.]

The next case keeps the public setting and leaves the container untouched. A technological device reveals what is inside. On the proposed criterion this too would be outside the category: no physical opening or interference has been introduced. Yet the device example makes that classification doubtful. One possible repair adds a condition excluding the use of such a device from the original permission to classify conduct as a non-search. In the book's discussion, the revised treatment of the X-ray separates the new case from the dog. The objection went to the scope of the classificatory rule. No one needed to deny the factual similarity on which the first inference depended. A reader can now distinguish three separate moves: classifying the first case under an articulated rule, transferring its treatment by analogy, and questioning that transfer by revising the rule. The materials explore a disputed argument and attribute the proposed qualification to Weinreb. They do not settle the present constitutional status of detection devices. [Walton, Reed & Macagno, ch. 2, pp. 75–81, figs. 2.1–2.2.]

A counterexample can also require less revision than first appears. In the book's essay-deadline exchange, a student invokes an extension previously granted to another student who had a competing assignment. If the deadline never covered the cited case, there is no exception to explain. If the previous extension was legitimate and already fell under a recognized exception, the issue is whether the new student qualifies under it. If it was legitimate, covered, and unaccounted for by an existing exception, the case presses for a qualification of the rule itself. Those replies treat the previous decision differently, even if each speaker agrees that it occurred. [Walton, Reed & Macagno, ch. 2, pp. 72–74.]

### 8. Revise conclusions when grounds or priorities change

A **rebutting** argument supports an opposing consequence. An **undercutting** argument blocks the passage from a premise to a conclusion without establishing the contrary conclusion. An exception can itself face an exception. Removing that obstacle may reinstate the original argument, but only if its essential premises and other applications survive. An ordinary defeasible inference from A to B does not license inferring the absence of A solely from the absence of B. Nor does silence about an exception prove that it did not occur. [Henry Prakken, *Logical Tools for Modelling Legal Argument* (1997), chs. 4–6, pp. 67–178, §6.5.1.]

Priorities sometimes resolve conflicts. A claim that the more recent or more specific rule prevails needs support within the relevant legal setting. The priority rule can itself be disputed. Equally ranked arguments can remain unresolved. An additional argument for the same outcome deserves attention both for its independence and for any vulnerability it shares with the first. An outcome supported by an independently surviving route differs from one that recurs under each of two alternative assumptions. In the latter case the result is conditional on those alternatives exhausting the possibilities, and neither underlying assumption becomes an established fact. These distinctions are informal uses of concepts from a formal theory. They confer no formal proof status on a lecture's conclusion. [Prakken, ch. 6, §6.5.1, Example 6.5.8 and Definition 6.5.9, ch. 7 §7.5.3, pp. 196–197, and ch. 8, pp. 203–220]

## Worked Example

**From a banking record to an allegation of intent.** In the *United States v. Able* exercise, the question concerns intentionally underreporting income. The provisional prosecution theory contrasts $125,000 of salary reported on a return with an additional $45,000 in fees. The endorsement by a Las Vegas branch on one $25,000 check from a law firm was offered in support of a possible motive. The figure on the exhibit and the total allegedly omitted are distinct. The endorsement matters to that theory only through a series of claims: the money was received there, it was gambled, the gambling produced a loss large enough to create pressure, and that pressure gave a motive to underreport. Arranging these claims in time does not establish the links. Receipt is compatible with uses other than gambling. Gambling can produce winnings. Even a proved loss and a motive do not establish what the taxpayer knew when filing.

A second issue concerns what the taxpayer disclosed to the accountant. The provisional defense theory supplies a precise alternative: a letter was delivered to the accountant's office disclosing the fees, together with a presigned return and a signed check on which the amount was blank. Delivery, content, and actual reliance matter separately. They are parts of that theory and require proof. Evidence about those communications bears on a different link to knowledge and intent. It can change the assessment without changing what the return says. A gambling record, conversely, could strengthen one part of the motive theory while leaving the communications entirely open. Prior returns illustrate how one item can work on both sides. They bear on historic income in the government's theory of financial pressure and on the regular past use of accountants in the defense theory of reliance. Discarding them as soon as one purpose fails would discard the other use without examining it. Putting the same returns in both chains does not create two independent pieces of evidence. These comparisons explain which additional fact would answer which objection. The exercise develops an evidential investigation and supplies no verdict. [Terence Anderson, David Schum & William Twining, *Analysis of Evidence*, 2nd ed. (2005), ch. 5, pp. 125–133, especially pp. 127, 129–132, *United States v. Able* exercise.]

## Decision Rules & Judgment

| Difficult judgment | What decides it |
|---|---|
| A fact is conceded but liability remains contested | Identify the remaining interpretation, classification, or ground of legal relevance. More proof of the conceded fact cannot resolve it. [MacCormick, chs. 3–4.] |
| A party is said to have a “right” | Identify the counterparty and the conduct or change at issue. A claim to performance, permission to act, and power to alter a relation have different correlatives. [Hohfeld, pp. 28–59.] |
| A citation or piece of evidence appears decisive | Identify which proposition it supports and which transition still needs a warrant. A source may support wording, an event, or a reported belief without supporting all three. [Toulmin, Essay III, pp. 89–100; Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 2, 11.] |
| An exception seems possible | Identify the consequence it would displace, whether its predicates have support, and the actual burden for this issue or stage. A supported exception to one route can leave another route intact. [MacCormick, ch. 12; Prakken, chs. 5–7.] |
| A superficially similar case has a different outcome | Compare the material reason for the earlier disposition, the identity of the decision-maker, and its authority to distinguish or depart. An outcome alone has limited value in reconstructing a holding. [Duxbury, chs. 3–4.] |
| A student or party has identified a genuine weakness | Determine whether it affects a premise, the inference, or a conclusion. A weakness in one argument does not establish an opponent's positive account. [Walton, Reed & Macagno, ch. 7; Prakken, §6.5.1 and Example 6.5.8, pp. 163–170.] |

## Key Takeaways

- Each important conclusion has the parties, conditions, and force of the premise that authorizes it. A move to a different remedy or institutional power requires further support. [MacCormick, chs. 3–4.]
- Fact-finding, interpretation, and proof of authority can stay open independently. Identifying the unresolved link determines whether more evidence, a legal source, or a narrower conclusion would help. [Hohfeld, pp. 25–28; Toulmin, Essay III; Anderson, Schum & Twining, chs. 2, 5.]
- Conflicting reasons can remain intelligible without a forced resolution. Scope, institutional force, the target of an objection, and any surviving independent ground determine what remains supported. [Duxbury, chs. 3–4; Prakken, chs. 5–8.]
