# Teaching design for a reconstructed lecture

**Format**: Five full books in PDF and searchable text | **Sections**: 6 teaching topics | **Depth**: study  
**Sources**: Ference Marton and Shirley Booth, *Learning and Awareness* (1997). Susan A. Ambrose and colleagues, *How Learning Works* (2010). Jeroen J. G. van Merriënboer, Paul A. Kirschner, and Jimmy Frèrejean, *Ten Steps to Complex Learning* (4th ed., 2025). Robin Alexander, *A Dialogic Teaching Companion* (2020). Richard E. Mayer and Logan Fiorella, eds., *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed., 2022). Page numbers refer to the printed editions unless marked PDF. ## Mental Model

A lesson has an object: something the student is to notice, understand, interpret, or do. A difficult passage often contains all the terms yet leaves out the relationship that makes its conclusion intelligible. The form of the missing explanation matters. A student who lacks the meaning of an ingredient, a student who cannot see how the ingredients fit, and a student who can follow a procedure only when told to use it face different problems. A contrast can reveal the decisive difference. A demonstration can show how to act on it. An objection can reveal the point at which the explanation still fails. [Marton and Booth, chs. 5, 7 and Epilogue. Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 95–100, 107–111. Alexander, ch. 6, pp. 114–121.]

An object of awareness, a factual explanation, a complete professional performance, and a reasoned exchange supply different kinds of evidence. A visually clear explanation can help a student see a relation. Knowing whether the student later recognized that relation unaided requires an observation with that purpose. [Marton and Booth, chs. 5–7. Van Merriënboer and colleagues, chs. 5 and 15. Alexander, ch. 7. Lindner, *Cambridge Handbook*, ch. 46.]

## Frameworks & Structure

### 1. What becomes visible in a contrast

The *referential* aspect of an experience concerns what the object means. The *structural* aspect concerns the parts in focus, the relationships among them, and the surrounding field against which they acquire their significance. A learner attending to a recurring visible feature may use the same vocabulary as someone attending to the structure that produces it. The reference to the whole matters because a local step can be performed correctly with little grasp of its purpose. [Ference Marton and Shirley Booth, *Learning and Awareness* (1997), ch. 5, especially pp. 91–95, and ch. 7 on the relation of parts to the whole.]

In the recursion material, three descriptions concern one object: a programming construct, repetition, and self-reference. Calling a procedure recursive identifies it by name. Saying that something happens again identifies a recurrence. Seeing the dependence on the procedure itself picks out another aspect. A correct label therefore leaves open which relationship the speaker has recognized. The distinctions came from the task and population studied. They describe variations in ways of understanding and give no basis for assigning a fixed personal type. [Ch. 4, example 4.2, pp. 71–73, and ch. 5, example 5.5, pp. 94–95.]

Variation makes an aspect discernible against a background that remains present. Simultaneous awareness matters: it is possible to notice each element at a different moment and still miss the relation between them. Comparing two apparently similar situations may expose a difference in what they permit. Comparing situations that look different may reveal the same structure. Controlling one variable is useful when that design isolates the relevant change, while a different object may require seeing several related aspects together. [Marton and Booth, Epilogue, pp. 208–210. Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 110–111, 118.]

Figures 2.7 and 2.8 represent different organizations of a particular reading. One follows the textual order. Another arranges the content thematically and hierarchically. The second represents a more complex understanding in that study because it brings otherwise separated relations together. It would be a different claim to infer that hierarchy is always the better representation. A task about the sequence of a process can require precisely the ordering that the first kind of representation preserves. Deep and surface approaches concern a relationship to a task and its demands. [Marton and Booth, chs. 2–3, figs. 2.7–2.8, after Säljö (1982), p. 25, PDF41.]

### 2. Where the missing knowledge or decision lies

Prior knowledge has at least four consequential properties. It may be available but inactive, insufficient for the step, inaccurate, or inappropriate in this setting. Activating a familiar rule helps when it applies. If the student is already applying that rule in the wrong setting, activating it again can reinforce the original difficulty. A missing prerequisite calls for enough explanation to make the next move possible. An isolated inaccuracy may respond to an explicit correction and evidence. A belief embedded in a broader explanatory model can survive a counterexample if the learner treats the instance as an exception. The alternative account then needs to explain the evidence that the old model had organized. [Susan A. Ambrose, Michael W. Bridges, Michele DiPietro, Marsha C. Lovett, and Marie K. Norman, *How Learning Works* (2010), ch. 1, pp. 15–38, especially pp. 24–25.]

A correction can supply a true sentence without dislodging the account that made the error plausible. In the seasons study discussed by Ambrose and colleagues, college students watched an explanation of axial tilt and then revised essays about the seasons. The fundamental explanations in the essays changed little. A *bridging analogy* takes another route. In Clement's secondary-school physics intervention, students accepted that a compressed spring pushes back but struggled to accept a table exerting a force on a book. Compressed foam provided the first bridge, flexible wood the second, and the solid tabletop the final comparison. The property carried forward was the support's response to a load. The strikingly different appearance of the spring and the table had concealed this relation. Each intermediate comparison shortened the inference the learner had to make. This design makes use of an accurate intuition within the learner's existing account. The study supplies no reason to assume every misconception has a convenient chain of intermediate cases. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 1, pp. 24–27, on Stein and Dunbar as described by Dunbar, Fugelsang, and Stein (2007), and on Brown and Clement's bridging work.]

Organization changes what can readily be retrieved and applied. The kinship material illustrates how the same knowledge admits different organizations suited to different purposes. A list may be enough for identification. A question about connections requires the relationships to be visible. More connections do not by themselves make an organization more useful. An expert's organization can be rich and efficient precisely where a novice needs an intermediate dependency made explicit. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 2, pp. 46–65, especially pp. 47 and 65.]

Component skills, integration, and conditional application are separate dimensions of mastery. A successful calculation can show that a procedure was executed. It leaves open whether the student would select the procedure for a new situation. Likewise, success on two separately cued operations leaves coordination under joint demands untested. Expert fluency can conceal where a choice was made at all. Recovering the choice, the evidence used to make it, and the condition under which it changes gives a worked demonstration explanatory force. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, fig. 4.1, pp. 95–96, 107 and 120.]

The problem-solving sequence in introductory statistics makes the hidden decision concrete. Before choosing an analysis, the student must identify the relevant variables and determine their types. In Lovett's study, weakness in those two component skills impaired selection of an appropriate analysis. A polished derivation that begins after naming the method can therefore leave out the very step at issue. The equivalent omission in a case study is to announce the expert recommendation without identifying the question, the parties' perspectives, the constraints on action, or the alternatives from which the recommendation was selected. Explaining the selection reveals what evidence would warrant a different method or recommendation. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 98–101, especially pp. 99–100.]

Transfer also depends on how examples are placed in relation to one another. In the management-student study of Loewenstein, Thompson, and Gentner (2003), one group analyzed each negotiation case separately. Another explicitly compared cases. The comparison required students to articulate the underlying features that made the cases analogous or different, which connected the cases to negotiation principles. A pair of cases can therefore pose a decision that a succession of two complete narratives leaves untouched: which reason carries from the first case to the second, and what must remain true for it to carry? A related teaching design compares similar-looking physics problems whose decisive principles differ, or changes a surface feature while holding the principle constant. A prompt naming the connection can make use of prior knowledge possible, but then the supported performance does not show spontaneous recognition. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 107–111 and 118–119.]

Useful feedback relates a particular performance to an intelligible criterion and leaves an opportunity to use the information. Extensive remarks on a final draft have limited value if the next assignment provides no opportunity to practice the same skill. Earlier, more frequent feedback can support another attempt, but task complexity, risk, and the student's existing knowledge affect what amount and timing are useful. A comment that identifies several errors still leaves work to do if the learner cannot tell which one matters most or what to change first. Feedback, subsequent revision, and successful independent work are three observations with different implications. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 5, opening writing-assignment dilemma, pp. 141–142 and 152.]

### 3. Keeping a complex task whole while changing support

Four-component instructional design (4C/ID) addresses performance that integrates knowledge, skills, and attitudes. A *whole learning task* keeps the goal and the coordination among its parts visible. A *nonrecurrent* aspect requires reasoned selection or problem solving. A *recurrent* aspect follows a rule under specified conditions. Which description fits can depend on the task, the goal, and existing competence. The difference affects both the information that serves a learner and when it is useful. [Jeroen J. G. van Merriënboer, Paul A. Kirschner, and Jimmy Frèrejean, *Ten Steps to Complex Learning*, 4th ed., chs. 2–3, PDF31–33, 42 and 61–63.]

| Component | Its work within the whole task |
|---|---|
| Learning tasks | Varied complete tasks preserve the decisions and coordination needed for performance. A worked demonstration, a partially completed task, and independent work can involve the same kind of task with different degrees of support. |
| Supportive information | Domain models explain what the objects are, how they are related, or how a process works. Systematic approaches offer phases and heuristics for reasoning through unfamiliar instances. A heuristic still requires a choice. |
| Procedural information | A recurrent action needs its prerequisite knowledge and a usable condition–action rule near the point of execution. Corrective feedback addresses a misapplication of that rule. Support may fade as execution becomes fluent. |
| Part-task practice | A component that requires high automaticity may need more repetition than whole tasks supply. Extra practice makes sense once the role of the component in the whole is clear. |

[Van Merriënboer and colleagues, chs. 2–4, 7–13, especially fig. 2.1, printed p. 17, PDF32, and PDF75, 105, 173, 205, 217–218, 235–248, 263, 277, 291 and 300.]

Making a task simpler can mean reducing its constraints while retaining the full question. If a workable simpler whole is unavailable, an accumulated sequence of parts may be appropriate. The connections among the parts still have to be learned. Within a task class, support can fade as cases become familiar. On entering a class with new sources of complexity, support can rise again. The amount of support therefore need not descend in one uninterrupted line over a course. [Ch. 6, PDF141 and 155, and fig. 2.1.]

The video-production material illustrates two distinct choices. In one task, production cannot move past certain stages without approval. In a completion task, the learner receives an unfinished video, script, or storyboard and has to finish it with assistance in studying the partial product. The approval constraint governs performance. The incomplete product changes how much of the whole task the learner must initially supply. Treating those devices as interchangeable would lose the purpose of each. [Ch. 4, printed p. 90, PDF105.]

**Product support and process guidance can vary independently.** The bakery-video task supplies a client brief, resources, a script, footage, and an edited promotional video. The finished product shows what counted as an acceptable outcome. Intermediate products reveal how the brief was translated into a script and then into shots. A modeling example also exposes decisions during production: an expert identifies the feature being attended to and explains how it bears on the next choice. With the same finished product, a reverse task asks which plausible client brief it might fulfill. That question elicits a connection between evidence and purpose that a chronological retelling of the production steps might conceal. Given a failed commercial whose intended audience understood it differently, the learner can also critique the route from brief to product or propose an alternative. The text presents that failure as an instructional possibility, without attributing a particular failure to the bakery. [Van Merriënboer and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 79–86, especially table 4.5, pp. 84–85.]

A process worksheet can make hidden professional judgment recoverable without treating the example's route as inevitable. In the book's legal-plea illustration, arranging the documents is followed by identifying the parties, the law potentially engaged, and the result sought. A separate inquiry considers the setting of the plea. The strategy then weighs those findings before deciding what belongs in the presentation. Merely arranging the file does not select an argument, and rehearsing delivery cannot supply a missing justification for that argument. The worksheet provides questions and heuristics. It need not compel one correct strategy for every file. [Van Merriënboer and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 87–90, especially table 4.6, p. 88, adapted from Nadolski and colleagues (2001).]

A performance standard specifies what counts as adequate under given conditions. Within this model, meeting the standards for an integrated task supplies evidence about the underlying knowledge and routines on the assumption that those standards capture them. Completion with substantial help and completion independently under different conditions support different conclusions. A lecture can present a complete reasoned demonstration without supplying any observation of the reader's own performance. [Chs. 5 and 15, printed pp. 117 and 322, PDF132, 337 and 339.]

### 4. How a question or objection changes the explanation

Dialogic teaching has six related principles: collective, supportive, reciprocal, deliberative, cumulative, and purposeful. A group can share an object of inquiry while members disagree about it. Support makes room to offer and revise a thought. Reciprocity gives another contribution a real consequence for the exchange. Deliberation demands grounds. Cumulative progress connects contributions into a line of reasoning, and purpose guides which mode of talk is appropriate. An instructional explanation can establish facts that make a later disagreement intelligible. An exchange can be talkative yet examine none of its reasons. [Robin Alexander, *A Dialogic Teaching Companion* (2020), ch. 7, pp. 130–132, 136 and 145.]

After a student's answer, the teacher's third turn can render a verdict and move on, or it can take up what the answer disclosed. There is a difference between a student who applied the wrong condition and one who accepts the condition but challenges its adequacy. Repeating the condition answers only the first difficulty. Clarifying the objection, requesting its grounds, or testing an alternative can address the second. A substantive counterexample may change the explanation. If the evidence permits competing readings, the disagreement can remain with the reasons on each side intact. [Alexander, ch. 6, pp. 114–121, and ch. 7, pp. 149–153.]

The fabric observation from the PRINDEP study exposes a difficulty that can disappear in a tidied transcript. Children looked at cloth through magnifying lenses for about twenty minutes. The teacher repeatedly invited observations. The children offered several, but each answer effectively returned them to the same invitation until time ran out and the teacher supplied the expected observation. Many pupils spoke and the wording appeared open. What the sequence disclosed was a prior choice about what counted as the answer. No subsequent turn examined the pupils' different observations or showed why the intended feature mattered. The episode illustrates why an analyst must inspect the response to an answer before classifying an exchange as open inquiry. It also limits what can be recovered from a transcript: identical written assent can accompany quite different intonation, pauses, and gestures. A reconstruction that invents enthusiasm or agreement would decide a question its evidence leaves open. [Alexander, ch. 2, pp. 16–18, especially p. 17, and ch. 6, pp. 116–118.]

The discussion moves associated with Michaels and O'Connor serve distinct purposes. Time to formulate a thought and a revoicing that invites confirmation help clarify a contribution. Restating another participant's idea can make listening visible. Asking for grounds or presenting a counterexample opens reasoning to examination. Explaining agreement, disagreement, or an addition can connect contributions. Which move fits depends on the source of the difficulty. Participation frequency, a fluent answer, or a short silence cannot alone establish the quality of someone's thinking. [Alexander, ch. 7, discussion of Michaels and O'Connor, printed pp. 151–153, PDF162–164, and social and epistemic norms, p. 156.]

### 5. Which representation helps, and what it can establish

In the cognitive theory of multimedia learning, learners select relevant words and images, organize them into representations, and integrate them with prior knowledge under limits on processing capacity. A picture can make an inferred relationship inspectable. Unrelated detail can consume resources while supplying no help in constructing that relationship. Effort required to understand the relation remains necessary. The theories in an edited handbook address different questions, so this account of processing is specific to Mayer's chapter. [Richard E. Mayer and Logan Fiorella, eds., *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning*, 3rd ed. (2022), Mayer, ch. 5, PDF79, 83 and 87.]

Split attention arises when two indispensable sources are separated and the reader must repeatedly search for the corresponding parts. Integrating a label with the feature it names can reduce that search. Redundancy raises a different question: what work does the second representation contribute when the first already suffices? The same annotation may assist someone unfamiliar with the material and become superfluous for someone who knows it. Highlighting can direct attention to an important relation, but highlighting everything leaves selection to the reader again. A cue that supplies the relationship can also alter what a test of independent discovery measures. [Ayres and Sweller, ch. 15, PDF221. Kalyuga and Sweller, ch. 16, PDF234. Kalyuga, ch. 13, PDF193. Van Gog, ch. 17, PDF243. Lindner, ch. 46, PDF583.]

Worked examples can reduce unproductive search when novices face an unfamiliar task. A student still needs to process why successive steps are justified. Evidence on fading worked steps is concentrated in algorithmic domains, which limits generalization to open-ended interpretation. Segmenting at meaningful points can support the construction of the whole. Introducing the names and characteristics of unfamiliar components can make the later explanation accessible. Neither tactic requires front-loading all of a discipline's vocabulary. Much of the research on coherence, signaling and contiguity uses short materials and near-term tests. Prior knowledge, complexity, pacing, and the need to integrate sources affect how far an effect can travel. [Fiorella and Mayer, ch. 14, table 14.1, PDF216. Renkl, ch. 18, PDF259. Mayer and Fiorella, ch. 19.]

Generating an explanation can involve drawing an inference that the provided account leaves unstated. Restating the conclusion need not produce that inference. An example or demonstration can be useful where background knowledge is insufficient for independent generation. In one scientific-text study, supplied pictorial summaries supported transfer better than learner-generated ones. Producing a drawing can introduce a competing demand. These conditions matter when choosing between giving a representation and asking someone to make one. Comprehending several written documents also involves knowing which source supports a proposition and how it relates to another account. A source conflict can affect interpretation even when each individual passage reads clearly. [Chi, ch. 32, printed p. 381, PDF402, 405 and 410. Leutner and Schmeck, ch. 30, PDF383. Rouet and Britt, ch. 44, PDF542.]

**Feedback can return attention to the step that needs reasoning.** A correctness signal tells the learner how an attempt turned out. Giving the correct answer supplies a target for comparison. Explanatory feedback identifies why the answer succeeds or fails. In a study of worked electrical-engineering problems, one condition supplied detailed step explanations immediately after an error. Another directed students on the first error to the analogous step in the worked solution and supplied the explanation after a second error. The latter group did better on the near and far transfer tests in that study. The first intervention had given students a useful judgment to make about two steps. In a separate picture-sequencing task, submitting the whole sequence for feedback supported better transfer than repeatedly trying positions with immediate acceptance or rejection. Why matters here: a sequence can be assembled by eliminating wrong placements without ever understanding the causal chain. Explanatory text can also go unused. A study of 566 sixth graders found no comprehension advantage from its feedback conditions, and failure to engage with the explanations was a proposed account of that result. Feedback timing depends on what reasoning the task calls for and what the learner can do with the information. [Cheryl I. Johnson and Matthew D. Marraffino, *Cambridge Handbook*, ch. 34, pp. 406–409, particularly pp. 407–408.]

### 6. Which documents agree, and why that distinction matters

A text set can contain four importantly different relations. **Complementation** adds a different piece of information needed for an integrated account. **Redundancy** repeats information already supplied. **Support** gives grounds for a claim, sometimes through an explicit citation. **Contradiction** places two incompatible claims or evaluations in issue. In the Panama example, learning that a railway crossed the isthmus and that the United States had legal control of it brings together complementary facts. Two reports that both mention the dispatch of a ship repeat a claim. A text that invokes a treaty for a claimed right introduces purported grounds whose applicability can be examined. Disagreement might concern the time an event occurred or whether a treaty justified it. Those are different questions even when both accounts describe the same event. [Jean-François Rouet and M. Anne Britt, *Cambridge Handbook*, ch. 44, pp. 526–529, especially table 44.3, p. 528.]

Keeping the links between sources and claims can make a disagreement intelligible without inventing a verdict. In the Paris-opera experiment discussed by Rouet and Britt, a critic reported an enthusiastic reception, while a lighting technician reported that half the audience had left before intermission. Another version made the critic's report of the reception consistent with the technician's observation. A generic statement that the audience had a mixed reaction would erase the experimental difference and turn a possible reconciliation into an established fact. A source-sensitive account can state what each person reported, why these reports create a tension, and what further evidence would decide which interpretation of the reception is justified. In the discrepant condition readers paid more attention to source phrases and recalled more source detail after a short interval. This supports a role for connecting claims to speakers in these tasks. It confers no general guarantee that a reader who sees conflicting claims will evaluate either source well. [Rouet and Britt, ch. 44, pp. 529–533, especially table 44.4, p. 531, on Braasch, Rouet, Vibert, and Britt (2012).]

## Worked Example

**How can a child correctly count ten passengers and still believe there are ten on one side of a train?** In Dagmar Neuman's work with Jenny, described in *Learning and Awareness*, ten wooden passengers sat in five pairs in a toy train. Jenny could say there were ten altogether. She also said that one file contained five and the other ten. A diagnosis of carelessness would hide the consistency of her answers. She had likewise ascribed five fingers to one hand and ten to the other. In an ordered count, five and ten name the places where the counting stops. A correct final number is compatible with attending to the last position. It leaves open whether the number represents the size of a set whose members can be regrouped.

The dolls carried ordinal labels. Jenny recognized two selected dolls as the second and seventh and could also identify that two dolls were present. The change of question mattered. Their positions in a lineup were different, yet together they made a set of two. In a later discussion she hesitated over whether two files of five were the same size, and direct confirmation that they were equal appeared to confuse her. Soon afterwards she again gave the familiar five-and-ten answer for her hands. A correct answer on one occasion or agreement with the teacher did not yet establish the part–whole relationship. [Marton and Booth, ch. 8, example 8.6, pp. 189–190, on Neuman (1994).]

Four days later, Neuman and Jenny alternately took dolls and recorded each one with a stroke on their respective sides of an equals sign. The ordinal labels within each share were mixed. Jenny could name which individual dolls she had and which ones Neuman had, while the two sets of tally marks showed five on each side. Neither person needed the particular doll wearing number five to possess five dolls. The set's size persisted across changes in which named individuals belonged to it. The two shares could be represented as five and five, and the very same ten passengers still made the whole.

What changed in the evidence of understanding? Jenny answered five for each of her hands, counted five plus five as ten, and, when the question moved to toes, examined one foot and gave five for the other and ten for both. These responses support the interpretation that Jenny could now coordinate ordinal position with cardinal number, and each subset with the whole. The new context gave the finger and doll answers some evidential depth. This was a child who had participated in repeated individual sessions and classroom activities. The new answers show when the change became visible in this task. Exactly when Jenny learned it, and how much the equals sign contributed, remain open. For the reader, the decisive demonstration is why the initial answer made sense, which feature the shared task varied, which relationship remained stable, and what later responses gave the interpretation more support. [Marton and Booth, ch. 8, pp. 189–192, especially pp. 190–191.]

## Decision Rules & Judgment

| Evidence in the material | Consequence for the explanation |
|---|---|
| A correct label conceals different ways of understanding the same object | Identify the relationship that distinguishes the readings. Keep enough of the whole problem present for the difference to matter. [Marton and Booth, chs. 4–5, 7 and Epilogue.] |
| Two cases look alike, yet the familiar method fails in the second | Identify the underlying principle and the feature that licenses its use. Varying a surface feature and varying a relevant condition test different judgments. [Ambrose and colleagues, ch. 4, pp. 107–111 and 118–119.] |
| The difficulty is an unfamiliar term, a failure to connect known parts, or a wrong choice of method | Supply the necessary prerequisite, expose the coordination, or contrast conditions of application, respectively. A single generic expansion cannot perform all three functions. [Ambrose and colleagues, chs. 1, 2 and 4.] |
| A complex task overwhelms the initial explanation | Simplify meaningful conditions, keep the goal recognizable, and supply information at the point its function requires. Restore support at a new level of complexity. [Van Merriënboer and colleagues, chs. 2–4, 6–7 and 10.] |
| A student's statement challenges the initial explanation | Revoice the contribution and leave room for correction, then examine the grounds of the objection. The next turn can settle whether the disagreement concerns the finding or its interpretation. [Alexander, chs. 6–7, pp. 116–118 and 150–153.] |
| A visual or worked example supplies the answer a student is supposed to find | It can be appropriate as guidance. An inference of independent selection requires a task where the critical relation was not already supplied. [Van Gog, ch. 17. Renkl, ch. 18. Lindner, ch. 46.] |

## Key Takeaways

1. A meaningful variation changes what can be discerned while leaving enough of the original situation available for comparison.
2. The missing step may concern background, organization, integration, or the conditions under which prior knowledge applies.
3. Whole tasks preserve coordination. Information, examples, and practice can be adjusted without losing sight of the whole.
4. A student's objection has educational force when it changes which reason must be explained or examined next.
5. A manuscript can display reasons, comparisons, and solutions. Claims about what a learner can later do require evidence about that learner's performance.
